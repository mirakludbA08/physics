Organic synthesis

This topic pulls the organic chemistry of the course together. It shows how to plan a route from one compound to another, how to lengthen a carbon chain with Grignard reagents, and the practical techniques used to make, separate, purify and identify an organic product safely.

Planning a synthetic route

To plan a synthesis, identify the functional group you start with and the one you need, then work out a sequence of known reactions that connects them, often working backwards from the target. For each step you must be able to give the reagents, the conditions and the type of reaction. Fewer steps usually means a higher overall yield, because some product is lost at every stage.

Aromatic routes

  • Benzene → nitrobenzene → phenylamine: concentrated HNO3 and H2SO4 below 55 °C; then tin and concentrated HCl, heat, followed by NaOH.
  • Phenylamine → azo dye: NaNO2 and HCl below 10 °C, then phenol in NaOH. Or phenylamine + an acyl chloride gives an N-phenyl amide.
  • Benzene → phenylethanone (CH3COCl with AlCl3), which then reacts like any ketone: LiAlH4 gives 1-phenylethanol, which can be esterified; HCN/KCN gives a hydroxynitrile, which hot HCl hydrolyses to a hydroxy acid.
  • Other ring reactions: Br2/FeBr3, a chloroalkane with AlCl3, SO3 in fuming sulfuric acid, and H2 over nickel (see Topic 18).

Grignard reagents

A Grignard reagent, RMgX, is made by warming a halogenoalkane with magnesium turnings in dry ether: CH3CH2Br + Mg → CH3CH2MgBr (ethylmagnesium bromide). The carbon bonded to magnesium carries a partial negative charge, so it acts as a nucleophilic carbon and forms new carbon–carbon bonds, lengthening the chain. Everything must be dry, because water destroys the reagent.

The Grignard reagent adds to the δ+ carbon of a C=O group; adding water (or dilute acid) afterwards gives the product.

Practical techniques

Heating under reflux and distillation

  • Reflux heats a reaction mixture for a long time without losing volatile substances: a vertical condenser turns vapours back to liquid, which drips back into the flask. It is used, for example, to oxidise a primary alcohol fully to a carboxylic acid with excess dichromate. Never seal the top of the condenser: pressure would build up and the apparatus could explode.
  • Distillation separates a liquid product from the mixture, collecting the fraction that boils near the product's boiling point; the thermometer bulb sits level with the side arm. Distilling the aldehyde off as it forms, with limited dichromate, stops it being oxidised further.
  • Anti-bumping granules make small bubbles form, preventing violent uneven boiling. Organic liquids are heated with electric heaters or water baths, as they are usually flammable.
  • When drawing apparatus: no lines across the joins between flask, still head and condenser; the condenser has an outer water jacket sealed at both ends, with water in at the bottom and out at the top.

Purifying an organic liquid

  1. Put the impure product in a separating funnel and shake it with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution to neutralise acid impurities, releasing the pressure of CO2 often. Shaking with saturated sodium chloride solution helps the layers separate.
  2. Let the layers separate and run off the aqueous layer. The organic layer is usually the less dense, upper layer, but check densities.
  3. Run the organic layer into a dry flask and add a drying agent, such as anhydrous calcium chloride or magnesium sulfate, until the liquid is clear. The drying agent must be insoluble in, and not react with, the product.
  4. Decant or filter the liquid off the drying agent and distil, collecting the fraction at the product's boiling point.

Steam distillation and solvent extraction

  • Steam distillation: steam is blown through the mixture and the product distils over with the water at below 100 °C, so a high-boiling compound that would decompose at its own boiling point can be separated. Phenylamine is purified this way.
  • Solvent extraction: shake the mixture with an organic solvent in which the product dissolves better than in water, separate the layers, dry the organic layer and distil off the solvent.

Checking purity

  • Melting point: a pure solid melts sharply at the value in data books; impurities lower the melting point and make it melt over a range. Use a melting-point apparatus, or a capillary tube strapped to a thermometer in an oil bath (the oil must boil above the sample's melting point). Heat slowly so the thermometer and sample stay at the same temperature.
  • Boiling point: measured with the thermometer bulb in the vapour just above the boiling liquid. It is less useful for identification, since different liquids can share a boiling point, and it changes with atmospheric pressure.

Hazards and risks

  • A hazard is anything with the potential to cause harm: toxic, flammable, harmful, irritant, corrosive, oxidising, carcinogenic. A risk is the chance that harm actually results, which depends on the amount, the concentration and how the substance is used.
  • Control measures match the hazard: eye protection for irritants and corrosives, gloves and a fume cupboard for toxic substances, no naked flames near flammable liquids, oxidising agents kept away from flammable materials.

Identifying the product

Combustion analysis

0.290 g of a compound of C, H and O burns completely to give 0.660 g of CO2 and 0.270 g of H2O. Find its empirical formula.

Carbon: moles of CO2 = 0.660 ÷ 44.0 = 0.0150 mol, so 0.0150 mol of C, mass 0.180 g.

Hydrogen: moles of H2O = 0.270 ÷ 18.0 = 0.0150 mol, so 2 × 0.0150 = 0.0300 mol of H, mass 0.030 g.

Oxygen by difference: 0.290 − 0.180 − 0.030 = 0.080 g, so 0.080 ÷ 16.0 = 0.0050 mol.

Ratio C : H : O = 0.0150 : 0.0300 : 0.0050 = 3 : 6 : 1, so the empirical formula is C3H6O. If the mass spectrum shows M+ at 58, this is also the molecular formula: propanone or propanal, which IR, NMR or chemical tests can tell apart.

The spectroscopic methods (mass spectra, IR and NMR) are covered in Topics 10 and 15, including a full worked identification.

Quick checks

Which sequence converts bromoethane into propanoic acid?

Cyanide adds a carbon (CH3CH2CN), and acid hydrolysis turns the nitrile into CH3CH2COOH. NaOH then oxidation would give ethanoic acid, with only two carbons.

Which product forms when CH3MgI reacts with propanone and the mixture is then treated with water?

A Grignard reagent with a ketone gives a tertiary alcohol: the CH3 joins the carbonyl carbon, giving (CH3)3COH.

Why is a primary alcohol oxidised under reflux to make a carboxylic acid, but distilled to make an aldehyde?

The aldehyde has a lower boiling point than the alcohol and acid, so distillation carries it away before it meets more oxidising agent.

In recrystallisation, why is the minimum volume of hot solvent used?

With too much solvent, much of the product stays dissolved when cold and the yield falls, or no crystals form at all.

A student's crystals melt between 118 °C and 124 °C; the data book value is 127 °C. What does this suggest?

A pure substance melts sharply at its data book value. A low, broad melting range points to impurities, such as remaining solvent.

Which statement about hazard and risk is correct?

A hazard is the potential to cause harm; risk is the chance that harm actually happens, which falls with smaller amounts, lower concentrations and good control measures.
