Entropy and energetics

Why does sodium chloride exist, why is it CaCl2 and not CaCl, and why do some reactions that take in heat still happen by themselves? This topic answers with two tools: Born–Haber and solution cycles, which measure the energy holding ionic solids together, and entropy, which counts the ways energy can be shared out and decides whether a change is feasible at all.

The enthalpy changes behind an ionic lattice

The lattice energy of an ionic compound measures how strongly its ions hold each other. It cannot be measured directly, because you cannot collect a mole of free gaseous ions and let them crash together. Instead it is calculated with Hess's law from enthalpy changes that can be measured. You need to know each of them precisely. All are standard values: measured at 100 kPa and a stated temperature, usually 298 K, with substances in their standard states.

  • Why is the first electron affinity negative? The incoming electron is attracted by the nucleus of the neutral atom, so energy is released. Going down group 7 from chlorine to iodine it becomes less exothermic: the atoms are bigger, with more shielding, so the added electron is held less strongly.
  • Why is the second electron affinity positive? The electron is being added to an ion that is already negative. Energy has to be put in to overcome the repulsion between them. For oxygen it is +798 kJ mol−1, much larger than the −141 kJ mol−1 released by the first.
  • Atomisation of a diatomic element is per mole of atoms formed, so ΔatH(Cl) is half the Cl–Cl bond enthalpy: about +122 kJ mol−1 from a bond enthalpy of about +243 kJ mol−1.

Lattice energy is defined here as the enthalpy change for forming the solid from gaseous ions, so it is negative. The reverse process, breaking the lattice apart, has the same size but a positive sign. Read every question carefully to see which direction it means, and always write state symbols: the ions must be gaseous.

Born–Haber cycles

A Born–Haber cycle is a Hess's law cycle that links the formation of an ionic solid directly from its elements with an indirect route through gaseous atoms and gaseous ions. Both routes start and finish in the same place, so their total enthalpy changes are equal:

Born–Haber cycle for a compound MX

\[ \Delta_\mathrm{f}H = \Delta_\mathrm{at}H(\mathrm{M}) + \text{IE}(\mathrm{M}) + \Delta_\mathrm{at}H(\mathrm{X}) + \text{EA}(\mathrm{X}) + \text{LE}(\mathrm{MX}) \]
IE: ionisation energy (or energies)EA: electron affinity (or affinities)LE: lattice energy

Data / kJ mol−1: ΔfH(NaCl) = −411; ΔatH(Na) = +107; first IE(Na) = +496; ΔatH(Cl) = +122; first EA(Cl) = −349.

Go round the indirect route from the elements to the gaseous ions: atomise sodium, ionise it, atomise chlorine, add the electron to chlorine.

\[ 107 + 496 + 122 + (-349) = +376\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \]

So Na+(g) + Cl−(g) sits 376 kJ mol−1 above the elements.

Hess's law: ΔfH = 376 + LE, so

\[ \text{LE} = \Delta_\mathrm{f}H - 376 = -411 - 376 = -787\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \]
  • Group 2 halides such as MgCl2: include both the first and second ionisation energies of the metal, and twice the atomisation enthalpy and electron affinity of chlorine, because there are two chloride ions. (2 × ΔatH(Cl) equals the Cl–Cl bond enthalpy.)
  • Oxides such as MgO: include the first and second electron affinities of oxygen. The second is endothermic, so its arrow points up in the cycle.
  • Any one value in the cycle can be found if all the others are known: exam questions often ask for an electron affinity or an atomisation enthalpy instead of the lattice energy.

When drawing a cycle, put the gaseous ions at the top, with every species and state symbol on each level, and include the electrons (e−) on the levels where they are free. Up arrows are endothermic and down arrows exothermic.

Why calcium chloride is CaCl2

Born–Haber cycles can test compounds that don't exist. A theoretical lattice energy can be estimated for CaCl and CaCl3 from the sizes and charges of the ions, and the cycle then predicts their enthalpies of formation.

The enthalpy of formation is largely a contest between the energy needed to make the cation and the energy released when the lattice forms. Going from Ca+ to Ca2+ costs an extra 1145 kJ mol−1, but the doubly charged ion attracts two chloride ions far more strongly, and the lattice energy grows by over 1500 kJ mol−1: CaCl2 is much more exothermic than CaCl. Removing a third electron would mean breaking into the full inner shell of calcium, which costs 4912 kJ mol−1. The extra lattice energy cannot repay that, so CaCl3 would be strongly endothermic. Even CaCl, though exothermic, would not survive: 2CaCl(s) → Ca(s) + CaCl2(s) releases about 440 kJ mol−1.

What makes a lattice energy large?

Lattice energy comes from the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. That attraction is stronger when the charges are larger and when the ions can get closer together. So lattice energy becomes more exothermic when:

  • the ions are smaller: their centres are closer, so they attract more strongly. Lattice energies become less negative down a group.
  • the ions have larger charges: the attraction depends on the product of the charges, so going from 1+ and 1− ions to 2+ and 2− ions roughly quadruples it.

Theoretical and experimental lattice energies

A lattice energy can also be calculated purely from theory, using the perfect ionic model: the ions are treated as perfect spheres with their charge spread evenly, held together only by electrostatic attraction. Comparing this theoretical value with the experimental value from a Born–Haber cycle tests whether the model is right.

  • If the two values agree closely, the compound is almost purely ionic.
  • If the Born–Haber value is more exothermic than the theoretical one, the bonding has some covalent character. The cation pulls the anion's electron cloud towards itself (polarisation), concentrating electron density between the ions; this extra attraction makes the real lattice stronger than the model predicts.
  • Polarising power of a cation is greatest when it is small and highly charged (high charge density): Mg2+ polarises more than Na+, and Al3+ more still.
  • Polarisability of an anion is greatest when it is large (and highly charged): its outer electrons are far from the nucleus and easily pulled. I− is far more polarisable than F−.

Enthalpies of solution and hydration

The enthalpy change of solution, ΔsolH, is the enthalpy change when 1 mol of an ionic solid dissolves in enough water that the ions are far apart and do not interact: NaCl(s) + aq → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq).

The enthalpy change of hydration, ΔhydH, is the enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous ions is dissolved in enough water that further dilution causes no further enthalpy change: Na+(g) + aq → Na+(aq).

Water molecules are polar. The δ− oxygen atoms cluster round positive ions and the δ+ hydrogen atoms point at negative ions. These ion–dipole attractions release energy, so hydration enthalpies are always negative.

Hydration enthalpy is more exothermic for ions with a higher charge density: a smaller radius or a larger charge lets the water molecules get closer and attract more strongly.

The solution cycle

Dissolving can be imagined in two stages: break the lattice into gaseous ions (the reverse of lattice formation, so +|LE|), then hydrate the ions. Hess's law gives:

Enthalpy change of solution

\[ \Delta_\mathrm{sol}H = -\text{LE} + \sum \Delta_\mathrm{hyd}H \]
LE: lattice energy (negative, for formation)\(\sum \Delta_\mathrm{hyd}H\): hydration enthalpies of all the ions

Data / kJ mol−1: LE(MgCl2) = −2524; ΔhydH(Mg2+) = −1920; ΔhydH(Cl−) = −364.

Breaking up the lattice is the reverse of lattice formation: +2524 kJ mol−1.

Hydrate one Mg2+ ion and two Cl− ions:

\[ \sum\Delta_\mathrm{hyd}H = -1920 + 2(-364) = -2648\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \]

Add the two stages:

\[ \Delta_\mathrm{sol}H = +2524 + (-2648) = -124\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \]

Exothermic: the hydration of the ions releases more energy than breaking the lattice takes in, and the solution warms up as magnesium chloride dissolves.

Lattice energy and total hydration enthalpy are usually large and similar in size, so ΔsolH, the small difference between them, can come out either side of zero. A very endothermic ΔsolH, where the lattice is far stronger than the hydration can repay, usually means the compound is insoluble. But enthalpy is not the whole story: ammonium nitrate dissolves readily even though ΔsolH is +26 kJ mol−1 and the solution goes cold. To see why, we need entropy.

Entropy

A spontaneous (feasible) change is one that happens on its own once started, without anything continuing to drive it: a gas spreading through a room, ice melting on a warm day, an acid neutralising an alkali. Most feasible reactions are exothermic, but not all. Ice melting and ammonium nitrate dissolving both take in heat. The quantity that decides the direction of every change is entropy, S.

Entropy is a measure of the number of ways that particles, and the quanta of energy they hold, can be arranged. The more ways there are, the greater the entropy, and the more disordered the system appears.

Energy comes in small packets (quanta) that particles can swap among themselves. With more quanta, or more particles to share them, the number of possible arrangements grows enormously. A system left to itself drifts into the most probable state: the one with the most arrangements, which is why energy spreads out and never concentrates by itself.

Entropy of substances

  • Gases have far higher entropies than liquids, and liquids higher than solids, because their particles are free to move and can be arranged in vastly more ways.
  • Entropy increases with temperature: more energy quanta means more ways to arrange them, and particles vibrate and move more.
  • There is a sudden jump at each change of state. The jump at boiling is much bigger than at melting, because a gas is so much more disordered than a liquid.
  • More complex molecules, with more atoms that can move in more ways, have higher entropies than simple ones; mixtures have higher entropies than pure substances.
  • Only a perfect crystal at 0 K has zero entropy. Every substance at 298 K, elements included, has a positive standard entropy, S⦵, measured in J K−1 mol−1 (note: joules, not kilojoules).

Predicting the sign of ΔSsystem

The system is the reacting chemicals; everything else (the flask, the water, the air) is the surroundings. You can usually predict the sign of the system's entropy change from the equation. ΔSsystem is positive when a solid or liquid turns into a gas, and when the number of moles of gas increases; it is negative when the reverse happens.

  • NH4Cl(s) → NH3(g) + HCl(g): a solid becomes two gases, so ΔSsystem is large and positive.
  • 2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s): a gas is used up to make a solid, so ΔSsystem is negative.
  • N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g): four moles of gas become two, so ΔSsystem is negative.
  • NaCl(s) + aq → Na+(aq) + Cl−(aq): an ordered lattice breaks into freely moving ions, so ΔSsystem is usually positive.

Calculating ΔSsystem

Entropy change of the system

\[ \Delta S^{\circ}_\text{system} = \sum S^{\circ}_\text{products} - \sum S^{\circ}_\text{reactants} \]
Multiply each standard entropy by its number of moles in the equationUnits: J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹

Data / J K−1 mol−1: S⦵[N2(g)] = 191.6; S⦵[H2(g)] = 130.6; S⦵[NH3(g)] = 192.3.

Products: 2 × 192.3 = 384.6. Reactants: 191.6 + 3 × 130.6 = 583.4.

\[ \Delta S_\text{system} = 384.6 - 583.4 = -198.8\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} \]

Negative, as predicted: four moles of gas become two.

Entropy of the surroundings and total entropy

An exothermic reaction releases energy into the surroundings. Those extra quanta can be shared among the surrounding particles in many more ways, so the entropy of the surroundings increases. An endothermic reaction takes energy from the surroundings and lowers their entropy. The effect is bigger when the surroundings are cold: adding a given amount of energy to something cold makes a larger relative difference to the number of ways it can be shared than adding it to something already hot.

Entropy change of the surroundings and total entropy change

\[ \Delta S_\text{surroundings} = -\frac{\Delta H}{T} \qquad\qquad \Delta S_\text{total} = \Delta S_\text{system} + \Delta S_\text{surroundings} \]
\(\Delta H\) in J mol⁻¹ (× 1000 from kJ)\(T\) in KEntropies in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹

A change is feasible (thermodynamically possible) if ΔStotal is positive. This is the second law of thermodynamics: the total entropy of the system and its surroundings increases in every spontaneous change.

CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g). Data: ΔfH / kJ mol−1: CaCO3 −1206.9, CaO −635.1, CO2 −393.5. S⦵ / J K−1 mol−1: CaCO3 92.9, CaO 39.7, CO2 213.6.

Enthalpy change: ΔH = (−635.1 − 393.5) − (−1206.9) = +178.3 kJ mol−1.

Entropy change of the system: ΔSsystem = (39.7 + 213.6) − 92.9 = +160.4 J K−1 mol−1.

Positive, because a gas is made from a solid.

Entropy change of the surroundings at 298 K, converting ΔH to J mol−1:

\[ \Delta S_\text{surroundings} = -\frac{178\,300}{298} = -598.3\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} \]

Total:

\[ \Delta S_\text{total} = +160.4 + (-598.3) = -437.9\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}} \]

Negative, so limestone does not decompose at room temperature.

At what temperature does it become feasible? At the boundary ΔStotal = 0, so ΔSsystem = ΔH/T:

\[ T = \frac{\Delta H}{\Delta S_\text{system}} = \frac{178\,300}{160.4} = 1110\ \mathrm{K} \]

Above about 1110 K (840 °C), ΔSsurroundings is small enough for the positive ΔSsystem to win. Lime kilns run hotter than this.

Changes of state

At its melting or boiling temperature, a substance is in equilibrium between the two states, and ΔStotal = 0. That makes T = ΔH/ΔSsystem a way to calculate the temperature of a change of state. For water boiling, ΔH = +40.7 kJ mol−1 and ΔSsystem = +109 J K−1 mol−1, so T = 40 700 / 109 = 373 K. Below 373 K, ΔSsurroundings is more negative than ΔSsystem is positive, and liquid water is the stable state; above it, the reverse.

How temperature affects feasibility

Since ΔStotal = ΔSsystem − ΔH/T, raising the temperature shrinks the −ΔH/T term. Whether that helps depends on the signs:

Gibbs energy

Chemists usually combine ΔH and ΔSsystem into one quantity that refers only to the system: the Gibbs energy change, ΔG.

Start from the total entropy change:

\[ \Delta S_\text{total} = \Delta S_\text{system} - \frac{\Delta H}{T} \]

Multiply every term by −T:

\[ -T\Delta S_\text{total} = \Delta H - T\Delta S_\text{system} \]

Define the right-hand side as the Gibbs energy change:

\[ \Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S_\text{system} = -T\Delta S_\text{total} \]

Because T is always positive, ΔG is negative exactly when ΔStotal is positive.

Gibbs energy change

\[ \Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S_\text{system} \]
\(\Delta G\), \(\Delta H\) in kJ mol⁻¹\(\Delta S\) in kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (÷ 1000 from J)\(T\) in K

A reaction is feasible when ΔG is negative. When ΔG = 0 the system is at the boundary, in equilibrium. A graph of ΔG against T is a straight line with intercept ΔH and gradient −ΔSsystem, and it crosses zero at T = ΔH/ΔSsystem.

ΔH = +178.3 kJ mol−1 and ΔSsystem = +160.4 J K−1 mol−1 = +0.1604 kJ K−1 mol−1.

At 298 K:

\[ \Delta G = 178.3 - 298 \times 0.1604 = +130.5\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \]

Positive: not feasible, as the entropy calculation showed.

At 1200 K:

\[ \Delta G = 178.3 - 1200 \times 0.1604 = -14.2\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \]

Negative: feasible at kiln temperatures.

Gibbs energy and equilibrium constants

ΔG is linked to the equilibrium constant K (Kc or Kp) of the reaction by

Gibbs energy and the equilibrium constant

\[ \Delta G = -RT\ln K \]
\(R\) = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, so ΔG here is in J mol⁻¹\(T\) in K

A negative ΔG means ln K is positive, so K > 1 and products predominate at equilibrium. The more negative ΔG, the larger K. A reaction with ΔG of about −60 kJ mol−1 at 298 K has K of about 1010: it goes essentially to completion. A positive ΔG gives K < 1, but not zero, so a little product still forms.

Feasible does not mean fast

ΔG and ΔStotal say whether a reaction can happen, not how quickly. A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen at room temperature has a hugely negative ΔG for forming water, yet nothing happens until a spark supplies the activation energy. Diamond should turn into graphite at room temperature (ΔG is negative), but the rate is immeasurably slow. Such systems are thermodynamically unstable but kinetically stable (kinetically inert): a high activation energy stops the feasible reaction from happening at a noticeable rate. Remember too that the calculations use standard data and assume ΔH and ΔS stay the same at other temperatures, which is only approximately true.

Entropy and solubility

Now return to dissolving. When an ionic solid dissolves, the ions leave an ordered lattice and spread through the solution, so ΔSsystem is usually positive.

  • If ΔsolH is negative, ΔSsurroundings is positive too, so ΔStotal is positive and the solid dissolves at any temperature.
  • If ΔsolH is positive, ΔSsurroundings is negative. The solid still dissolves if the positive ΔSsystem is bigger. Ammonium nitrate: ΔsolH = +25.7 kJ mol−1, ΔSsystem = +108.7 J K−1 mol−1. At 298 K, ΔSsurroundings = −25 700/298 = −86.2, so ΔStotal = +22.5 J K−1 mol−1: it dissolves, and cold packs use exactly this.
  • Raising the temperature makes the negative −ΔsolH/T term smaller in size, so salts with an endothermic ΔsolH generally become more soluble when heated.
  • If ΔsolH is very endothermic, because the lattice energy is far larger than the hydration enthalpies, ΔSsurroundings is too negative to overcome and the solid is insoluble.

Quick checks

Which equation represents the lattice energy of magnesium oxide?

Lattice energy is the formation of 1 mol of solid from its gaseous ions. The first option is the enthalpy of formation; the second is the lattice breaking up (same size, opposite sign); aqueous ions are wrong because hydration energy would be involved.

Use the data to find the first electron affinity of bromine. Data / kJ mol−1: ΔfH(KBr) = −394; ΔatH(K) = +89; first IE(K) = +419; ΔatH(Br) = +112; LE(KBr) = −689.

ΔfH = ΔatH(K) + IE + ΔatH(Br) + EA + LE, so EA = −394 − (89 + 419 + 112 − 689) = −394 − (−69) = −325 kJ mol−1. A positive answer comes from a sign error with the lattice energy.

The Born–Haber lattice energy of a compound is much more exothermic than its theoretical lattice energy. What does this show?

The theoretical value assumes perfectly spherical ions. A polarising cation distorts the anion, putting electron density between the ions; this covalent character strengthens the lattice beyond what the ionic model predicts.

Which change has the largest positive ΔSsystem?

Only the decomposition of calcium carbonate creates a gas from a solid. The other three all reduce the amount or freedom of gas particles, so their ΔSsystem is negative.

A reaction has ΔH = −120 kJ mol−1 and ΔSsystem = −150 J K−1 mol−1. Above what temperature is it no longer feasible?

Feasible while ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is negative. ΔG = 0 when T = ΔH/ΔS = −120 000 / −150 = 800 K. Above that, −TΔS (positive) outweighs the negative ΔH. Forgetting to convert kJ to J gives 0.8 K.

Why is the hydration enthalpy of Mg2+ much more exothermic than that of Na+?

Hydration energy comes from ion–dipole attraction. Mg2+ has a much higher charge density than Na+ (twice the charge, smaller radius), so water's δ− oxygen atoms are pulled in closer and more strongly. Cations are surrounded by oxygen, not hydrogen, atoms.

The conversion of diamond into graphite has a negative ΔG at room temperature, yet diamonds do not change. Why?

A negative ΔG means ΔStotal is positive and K > 1, so the change is feasible. Feasibility says nothing about rate: diamond is kinetically stable because the activation energy for rearranging its strong covalent network is enormous.
