Acid-base equilibria

Acids and bases are all about protons moving from one particle to another. This topic turns that idea into numbers: the pH scale, the ionic product of water, the acid dissociation constant Ka, the shapes of titration curves, and the buffers that keep the pH of your blood almost perfectly steady.

Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases

  • A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton (H+) donor.
  • A Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor. It needs a lone pair of electrons to bond to the proton.

An acid can only give away a proton if something accepts it, so acid–base reactions always involve two pairs. When an acid loses its proton, what is left can accept a proton back: it is the acid's conjugate base. Likewise a base that gains a proton becomes its conjugate acid. A conjugate pair differs by exactly one H+.

Water acts as a base towards HCl but as an acid towards NH3: a substance that can do both is called amphoteric. In the last example nitrous acid is forced to act as a base, because nitric acid is the stronger acid; when two acids are mixed, the one with the larger Ka donates the proton.

pH and strong acids

\[ \mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}[\mathrm{H^+}] \qquad [\mathrm{H^+}] = 10^{-\mathrm{pH}} \]

The pH scale is logarithmic so that hydrogen-ion concentrations ranging over many powers of ten fit onto a scale of roughly 0 to 14. Each fall of one pH unit means ten times more H+. Give pH values to two decimal places.

A strong acid is fully dissociated in water, so for a monoprotic strong acid such as HCl or HNO3, [H+] equals the concentration of the acid.

0.0250 mol dm−3 nitric acid: [H+] = 0.0250 mol dm−3, so pH = −log 0.0250 = 1.60.

A hydrochloric acid solution has pH 2.45: [H+] = 10−2.45 = 3.55 × 10−3 mol dm−3, and that is also the concentration of the acid.

The ionic product of water, Kw

Water itself ionises very slightly: H2O(l) ⇌ H+(aq) + OH−(aq). So little water ionises that [H2O] is effectively constant, and it is folded into the constant to give the ionic product of water:

\[ K_w = [\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{OH^-}] = 1.00 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm{mol^2\,dm^{-6}}\ \text{at 298 K} \qquad \mathrm{p}K_w = -\log K_w = 14.00 \]

Kw applies to every aqueous solution, so if you know [OH−] you can find [H+], and the other way round.

Neutral water is not always pH 7

A solution is neutral when [H+] = [OH−]. At 298 K that gives [H+] = √(1.00 × 10−14) = 1.00 × 10−7 mol dm−3 and pH 7.00. The ionisation of water is endothermic, so warming it increases Kw. At 40 °C, Kw = 2.92 × 10−14 mol2 dm−6, so [H+] = √(2.92 × 10−14) = 1.71 × 10−7 mol dm−3 and pH = 6.77. That water is still neutral, because [H+] and [OH−] are still equal; neutral simply has a lower pH at higher temperatures.

pH of strong bases

Strong bases such as NaOH are fully dissociated, so 0.0500 mol dm−3 NaOH has [OH−] = 0.0500 mol dm−3.

Use Kw: \( [\mathrm{H^+}] = \dfrac{K_w}{[\mathrm{OH^-}]} = \dfrac{1.00 \times 10^{-14}}{0.0500} = 2.00 \times 10^{-13}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}} \).

pH = −log(2.00 × 10−13) = 12.70.

Watch for bases that release two hydroxide ions: 0.0200 mol dm−3 Ba(OH)2 gives [OH−] = 0.0400 mol dm−3 and pH 12.60.

Weak acids and Ka

A weak acid is only slightly dissociated: the solution is an equilibrium mixture that is mostly undissociated HA. Its strength is measured by the acid dissociation constant, Ka, which is simply Kc for the dissociation:

\[ \mathrm{HA(aq)} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{H^+(aq)} + \mathrm{A^-(aq)} \qquad K_a = \frac{[\mathrm{H^+}][\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]} \qquad \mathrm{p}K_a = -\log K_a \]
  • The units of Ka are mol dm−3.
  • The larger Ka (the smaller pKa), the further the equilibrium lies to the right and the stronger the acid. Ethanoic acid has Ka = 1.7 × 10−5 mol dm−3, pKa = 4.77.
  • Strong and weak are not the same as concentrated and dilute. Strength is about how much the acid dissociates; concentration is about how much acid is in each dm3.

Calculating the pH of a weak acid

Two approximations make the calculation simple:

  1. [H+] = [A−], because each HA that dissociates gives one of each. (This ignores the tiny amount of H+ from the water.)
  2. [HA] at equilibrium ≈ the concentration of acid you started with, because so little of it dissociates.

Then \( K_a = \dfrac{[\mathrm{H^+}]^2}{[\mathrm{HA}]_{\text{initial}}} \), so \( [\mathrm{H^+}] = \sqrt{K_a \times [\mathrm{HA}]_{\text{initial}}} \). The second approximation breaks down for acids that are fairly strong (large Ka) or very dilute, because then a significant fraction of the acid dissociates.

\( [\mathrm{H^+}]^2 = K_a \times [\mathrm{HA}] = 1.7 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.0500 = 8.5 \times 10^{-7} \)

\( [\mathrm{H^+}] = \sqrt{8.5 \times 10^{-7}} = 9.22 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}} \)

pH = −log(9.22 × 10−4) = 3.04.

A strong acid of the same concentration would have pH 1.30. Only about 2% of the ethanoic acid molecules are dissociated.

0.200 mol dm−3 propanoic acid has pH 2.79. Find Ka and pKa.

[H+] = 10−2.79 = 1.62 × 10−3 mol dm−3 = [A−].

\( K_a = \dfrac{(1.62 \times 10^{-3})^2}{0.200} = 1.32 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}} \), so pKa = 4.88.

Diluting acids

Diluting a strong acid ten times divides [H+] by ten, so the pH rises by exactly 1. Diluting a weak acid ten times raises its pH by only about 0.5: 0.0500 mol dm−3 ethanoic acid has pH 3.04, but 0.00500 mol dm−3 has pH 3.54. Adding water shifts the dissociation equilibrium to the right, so a larger fraction of the acid dissociates and partly makes up the lost H+. Mathematically, [H+] is proportional to the square root of the concentration.

Core practical: finding Ka of a weak acid. Calibrate a pH meter with buffers of known pH. Titrate 25.0 cm3 of the weak acid with sodium hydroxide to find the volume needed for neutralisation. Then add exactly half that volume of alkali to a fresh 25.0 cm3 of acid and measure the pH. At half-neutralisation [HA] = [A−], so pH = pKa (explained below).

Titration curves

A titration curve plots pH against the volume of one solution added to the other. To record one, put a known volume of acid in a beaker with a calibrated pH meter, add the alkali from a burette a little at a time, stirring and recording the pH after each addition, with smaller additions near the end point. The equivalence point is where the acid and base have reacted exactly in the ratio of the equation; on the graph it is the middle of the steep, nearly vertical part.

The pH at equivalence is not always 7 because the salt formed can itself react with water. Sodium ethanoate contains ethanoate ions, the conjugate base of a weak acid, which take protons from water and leave OH−: the solution is alkaline. Ammonium chloride contains NH4+, a weak acid, so its solution is acidic.

Half-neutralisation: pH = pKa

On the curve for a weak acid with a strong base, the pH rises quickly at first and then flattens into a buffer region. Halfway to the equivalence point, exactly half of the acid has been turned into its salt, so [HA] = [A−]. These cancel in the Ka expression, leaving Ka = [H+], so pH = pKa. This is the basis of the core practical above.

Choosing an indicator

An indicator is itself a weak acid, HIn, whose undissociated form and conjugate base have different colours: HIn ⇌ H+ + In−. Adding acid pushes the equilibrium left, giving the colour of HIn; adding alkali removes H+ and pulls it right, giving the colour of In−. The end point, where the colour changes, is when [HIn] = [In−]; it happens over a range of about 2 pH units centred on the indicator's pKin.

A suitable indicator changes colour completely within the steep part of the titration curve, so that one drop of titrant takes it all the way through its colour change at the equivalence point.

For a weak acid with a weak base there is no steep section, so no indicator gives a sharp end point; use a pH meter instead.

Diprotic acids such as ethanedioic acid, H2C2O4, lose their two protons one at a time, so their curve with sodium hydroxide has two steep sections and two equivalence points. In the same way, adding acid to sodium carbonate gives two steps: CO32− + H+ → HCO3−, then HCO3− + H+ → H2O + CO2.

Buffer solutions

A buffer is a solution whose pH changes very little when small amounts of acid or alkali are added, or when it is diluted.

  • Acidic buffer: a weak acid and a salt of that acid, for example ethanoic acid and sodium ethanoate. It holds a pH below 7.
  • Basic buffer: a weak base and a salt of that base, for example ammonia and ammonium chloride. It holds a pH above 7.

How an acidic buffer works

The buffer contains large reservoirs of both the weak acid, CH3COOH, and its conjugate base, CH3COO−, which comes almost entirely from the salt: CH3COOH ⇌ CH3COO− + H+.

  • Add a little acid: the added H+ combines with the large supply of ethanoate ions, CH3COO− + H+ → CH3COOH, so the equilibrium shifts left and almost all the added H+ is removed.
  • Add a little alkali: the OH− reacts with H+ to make water, and the equilibrium shifts right as more ethanoic acid dissociates to replace it. The OH− is also removed directly: CH3COOH + OH− → CH3COO− + H2O.
  • Either way the ratio [CH3COOH] / [CH3COO−] changes only slightly, because both reservoirs are large, so [H+] and the pH barely change.

Calculating the pH of a buffer

Rearranging Ka: \( [\mathrm{H^+}] = K_a \times \dfrac{[\mathrm{HA}]}{[\mathrm{A^-}]} \). Take [HA] as the acid added and [A−] as the salt added; the tiny amounts that dissociate or react with water make no noticeable difference. Because acid and salt share the same final volume, you can use their moles directly in the ratio.

50.0 cm3 of 0.200 mol dm−3 ethanoic acid is mixed with 50.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol dm−3 sodium ethanoate. Ka = 1.7 × 10−5 mol dm−3.

Moles of acid = 0.0500 × 0.200 = 0.0100 mol. Moles of ethanoate = 0.0500 × 0.100 = 0.00500 mol.

\( [\mathrm{H^+}] = 1.7 \times 10^{-5} \times \dfrac{0.0100}{0.00500} = 3.4 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}} \), so pH = 4.47.

50.0 cm3 of 0.400 mol dm−3 ethanoic acid is mixed with 30.0 cm3 of 0.200 mol dm−3 NaOH. Find the pH.

Moles of acid = 0.0200 mol; moles of NaOH = 0.00600 mol. The NaOH reacts completely: CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O.

Left over: 0.0200 − 0.00600 = 0.0140 mol of acid; formed: 0.00600 mol of ethanoate.

\( [\mathrm{H^+}] = 1.7 \times 10^{-5} \times \dfrac{0.0140}{0.00600} = 3.97 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}} \), so pH = 4.40.

1.00 dm3 of buffer contains 0.100 mol of ethanoic acid and 0.100 mol of sodium ethanoate, so its pH = pKa = 4.77. Now 0.0100 mol of HCl is added.

The H+ turns ethanoate into ethanoic acid: acid becomes 0.110 mol, ethanoate becomes 0.0900 mol.

\( [\mathrm{H^+}] = 1.7 \times 10^{-5} \times \dfrac{0.110}{0.0900} = 2.08 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}} \), so pH = 4.68, a fall of only 0.09.

The same acid added to 1.00 dm3 of pure water would take the pH from 7.00 to 2.00.

Buffers in blood

Blood plasma must stay between pH 7.35 and 7.45. The main buffer is carbonic acid and hydrogencarbonate ions: H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3−. Extra acid from metabolism is taken up by HCO3−, shifting the equilibrium left; extra alkali removes H+, and more H2CO3 dissociates to replace it. The carbonic acid level is itself controlled by breathing out carbon dioxide (H2CO3 ⇌ H2O + CO2). Buffers are also used in foods, shampoos and medicines to keep the pH where the product works best.

Enthalpy change of neutralisation

The standard enthalpy change of neutralisation is the enthalpy change when an acid and an alkali react under standard conditions to form one mole of water. For any strong acid with any strong alkali it is almost the same, about −57 kJ mol−1, because both are fully ionised and the only reaction is H+(aq) + OH−(aq) → H2O(l). With a weak acid (or weak base) it is slightly less exothermic, for example about −56 kJ mol−1 for ethanoic acid, because some energy is used up dissociating the acid as the reaction proceeds.

Quick checks

In HSO4− + H2O ⇌ SO42− + H3O+, which is the conjugate base of HSO4−?

HSO4− donates a proton and becomes SO42−; the pair differs by one H+. H3O+ is the conjugate acid of water.

What is the pH of 0.0150 mol dm−3 potassium hydroxide at 298 K?

[H+] = 1.00 × 10−14 ÷ 0.0150 = 6.67 × 10−13 mol dm−3, so pH = 12.18. 1.82 is the pOH, which is what you get if you take −log[OH−] and stop.

Pure water at 60 °C has pH 6.51. Which statement is correct?

In pure water [H+] always equals [OH−], so it is neutral. Ionisation of water is endothermic, so Kw rises with temperature and the neutral pH falls below 7.

What is the pH of 0.100 mol dm−3 of a weak acid with Ka = 1.0 × 10−5 mol dm−3?

[H+] = √(1.0 × 10−5 × 0.100) = 1.0 × 10−3 mol dm−3, so pH = 3.00. 1.00 would be right only for a strong acid. 6.00 comes from forgetting the square root.

Which indicator is suitable for titrating ammonia solution with hydrochloric acid?

A strong acid with a weak base has its steep section in the acidic region and an equivalence point near pH 5, which suits methyl orange. Phenolphthalein would change colour long before the equivalence point.

25.0 cm3 of a weak acid needs 24.0 cm3 of NaOH for neutralisation. After 12.0 cm3 of NaOH has been added the pH is 4.20. What is Ka?

12.0 cm3 is half the neutralisation volume, so pH = pKa = 4.20 and Ka = 10−4.20 = 6.3 × 10−5 mol dm−3.

Which mixture is a buffer?

A buffer needs a weak acid or weak base together with its conjugate partner. Ethanoic acid and NaOH in a 1 : 1 ratio react completely, leaving only sodium ethanoate; with excess ethanoic acid the mixture would be a buffer.

Why is the enthalpy change of neutralisation of ethanoic acid with NaOH slightly less exothermic than that of HCl with NaOH?

Both reactions form one mole of water per mole of acid, but ethanoic acid is mostly undissociated, and breaking the O–H bond to release its H+ takes in some energy.
