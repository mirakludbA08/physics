Superposition & Stationary Waves

What happens when two waves meet? They simply add up, point by point. That one idea, superposition, explains the loud and quiet spots between two loudspeakers, the bright and dark bands in a ripple tank, and the stationary waves on a guitar string that decide which notes it can play. We'll build it all up from the idea of phase.

Phase and phase difference

Every particle on a progressive wave oscillates with the same frequency, but they don't all reach the top at the same moment. A point a little further along the wave does exactly what the point behind it did, just slightly later. Phase is the word for "where a point is in its cycle", and we measure it as an angle: one complete cycle is \(2\pi\) radians, or 360°.

Phase, phase difference and wavefronts

  • Phase: the position of a point within its cycle of oscillation, given as an angle (one cycle = \(2\pi\) rad = 360°).
  • Phase difference: the fraction of a cycle by which one oscillation is ahead of or behind another, expressed as an angle in radians or degrees.
  • In phase: phase difference of zero (or a whole number of cycles: \(2\pi, 4\pi, \dots\)). Antiphase: phase difference of \(\pi\) rad (180°): one point is at a crest when the other is at a trough.
  • Wavefront: a line or surface joining points on a wave that are in phase, for example all the points along one crest. Wavefronts are usually drawn one wavelength apart (crest to crest), and the wave travels at right angles to them.

Along a progressive wave the phase changes steadily with distance. Walk one whole wavelength along the wave and you have gone through one complete cycle, so you are back in phase. That means the phase difference between two points depends only on what fraction of a wavelength apart they are. Exactly the same rule applies to two waves arriving at one point after travelling different distances: the extra distance one of them travels (the path difference) shifts its phase in the same way.

Phase difference from path difference

\[ \phi = \frac{2\pi\,\Delta x}{\lambda} \qquad\text{or}\qquad \phi = 360^\circ \times \frac{\Delta x}{\lambda} \]
\(\phi\): phase difference / rad (or °)\(\Delta x\): separation along the wave, or path difference / m\(\lambda\): wavelength / m

One wavelength along a progressive wave corresponds to one complete cycle of phase:

\[ \Delta x = \lambda \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \phi = 2\pi \text{ rad} \]

A distance \(\Delta x\) is the fraction \(\Delta x/\lambda\) of a wavelength, so it gives the same fraction of a full cycle:

\[ \frac{\phi}{2\pi} = \frac{\Delta x}{\lambda} \]

Multiply both sides by \(2\pi\):

\[ \boxed{\phi = \frac{2\pi\,\Delta x}{\lambda}} \]

Check the special cases: \(\Delta x = \lambda/2\) gives \(\phi = \pi\) (antiphase); \(\Delta x = \lambda\) gives \(2\pi\) (back in phase).

Give phase differences in radians as multiples of \(\pi\): \(\pi/2\), \(\pi\), \(3\pi/2\). If two points are more than one wavelength apart, take off whole wavelengths first: a separation of \(1.25\lambda\) gives the same phase difference as \(0.25\lambda\), which is \(\pi/2\). Mark schemes accept "antiphase", "\(\pi\) rad" or "180°".

Worked example

A progressive wave has frequency 25 Hz and speed 15 m s⁻¹. Points P and Q lie 0.45 m apart along the direction the wave travels.
(a) Calculate the phase difference between P and Q.
(b) State the smallest distance from P to a point that oscillates in antiphase with P.

Show solution

(a) First find the wavelength from \(v = f\lambda\):

\[ \lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{15}{25} = 0.60\ \text{m} \]

Then the phase difference:

\[ \phi = \frac{2\pi\,\Delta x}{\lambda} = \frac{2\pi \times 0.45}{0.60} = 1.5\pi\ \text{rad} = \frac{3\pi}{2}\ \text{rad}\ (270^\circ) \]

Q is further along, so Q lags P by \(3\pi/2\) rad. (You could equally say Q leads P by \(\pi/2\) rad: lagging by three quarters of a cycle looks the same as leading by one quarter.)

(b) Antiphase means \(\phi = \pi\), which is half a wavelength: \(\lambda/2 = 0.30\ \text{m}\).

The principle of superposition

Drop two stones in a pond and the ripples pass straight through each other, carrying on afterwards as if nothing had happened. While they overlap, though, the water surface has to do something. The rule is beautifully simple: each wave tries to displace the water, and the actual displacement is the sum of the two.

Principle of superposition

When two or more waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement at that point is the (vector) sum of the displacements that each wave would cause on its own.

  • Constructive superposition: the waves arrive in phase, crest on crest. The amplitudes add: \(A = A_1 + A_2\).
  • Destructive superposition: the waves arrive in antiphase, crest on trough. The amplitudes subtract: \(A = |A_1 - A_2|\). If the amplitudes are equal, the waves cancel completely.
  • Any other phase difference gives something in between.

Displacements add with their signs. A displacement of +1.2 cm from one wave and −0.8 cm from the other gives +0.4 cm, not 2.0 cm. And destructive interference doesn't destroy energy: where one region is quiet, the energy turns up in the loud regions nearby.

Beats: superposing two slightly different frequencies

If the two waves have slightly different frequencies, their phase difference keeps drifting. They slide in and out of step: in phase (loud), then antiphase (quiet), then in phase again. You hear a steady note whose loudness rises and falls at the beat frequency \(f_{\text{beat}} = |f_1 - f_2|\). Tuning forks of 440 Hz and 444 Hz played together give 4 beats per second. Musicians tune by adjusting one string until the beats slow down and vanish. Choose "Slightly different" in the simulation above to see the envelope (dashed) that produces the beats.

Coherence, path difference and interference

Superposition happens whenever waves meet, but usually you don't notice: the pattern of loud and quiet spots shifts around so quickly that it averages out. To see a steady pattern, the phase difference between the two sources must stay the same. That is what coherence means.

Coherence, path difference and interference

  • Coherent sources: sources that have the same frequency and a constant phase difference.
  • Path difference: the difference between the distances travelled by two waves from their sources to a given point.
  • Interference: the superposition of waves from coherent sources, which produces a stable pattern of maxima (constructive) and minima (destructive).

The easiest way to get coherent sources is to make one source do the job of two: two loudspeakers driven by the same signal generator, two dippers on the same vibrating bar in a ripple tank, or laser light passing through two slits. Two separate light bulbs are never coherent. Each atom emits light in short, random bursts, so the phase difference between the bulbs changes randomly and extremely rapidly, and any pattern washes out.

Now think about a point P in front of two coherent sources that are in phase. Whatever happens at P depends on how much further one wave has travelled than the other. Using \(\phi = 2\pi\Delta x/\lambda\):

Conditions for maxima and minima (sources in phase)

Path differencePhase difference at PResult
\(0,\ \lambda,\ 2\lambda,\ \dots = n\lambda\)\(0,\ 2\pi,\ 4\pi \dots\) (in phase)constructive: maximum
\(\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda,\ \tfrac{3}{2}\lambda,\ \dots = (n + \tfrac{1}{2})\lambda\)\(\pi,\ 3\pi \dots\) (antiphase)destructive: minimum

If the two sources are themselves in antiphase, everything swaps over: a path difference of \(n\lambda\) gives a minimum and \((n+\tfrac12)\lambda\) gives a maximum.

For a minimum to be completely silent (or completely dark), the two waves must also have equal amplitudes when they arrive, and transverse waves such as light must be polarised in the same plane. In real experiments the minima are usually just quieter, not silent, because the waves have spread out by different amounts and walls reflect extra sound in.

Worked example

Two loudspeakers A and B are connected to the same signal generator and emit sound in phase at 850 Hz. A microphone is 2.60 m from A and 3.20 m from B. The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹.
(a) Show that the microphone is at a minimum.
(b) The frequency is increased slowly. Find the next frequency at which the microphone detects a maximum.
(c) Suggest why the sound at the minimum does not drop to zero.

Show solution

(a) Wavelength: \(\lambda = v/f = 340/850 = 0.400\ \text{m}\). Path difference: \(3.20 - 2.60 = 0.60\ \text{m}\). In wavelengths:

\[ \frac{\Delta x}{\lambda} = \frac{0.60}{0.400} = 1.5 \]

A path difference of \(1.5\lambda\) means the waves arrive in antiphase (\(\phi = 3\pi\)), so there is destructive interference: a minimum.

(b) A maximum needs \(\Delta x = n\lambda\), so \(\lambda = 0.60/n\). Increasing \(f\) makes \(\lambda\) smaller than 0.400 m. The next allowed value is \(n = 2\):

\[ \lambda = \frac{0.60}{2} = 0.30\ \text{m} \quad\Rightarrow\quad f = \frac{v}{\lambda} = \frac{340}{0.30} = 1130\ \text{Hz}\ (3\ \text{s.f.}) \]

(c) The microphone is further from B, so the wave from B has spread out more and has a smaller amplitude than the wave from A. Unequal amplitudes can't cancel completely. Sound reflected off the walls and floor also reaches the microphone.

A point the same distance from both sources (path difference zero) is a maximum only if the sources are in phase. If they are in antiphase, the waves arrive crest on trough and the centre line is a minimum.

Young's double slit and \(\lambda = ax/D\)

Shine laser light through two narrow slits a distance \(a\) apart and you see evenly spaced bright fringes on a screen a distance \(D\) away. This was the experiment that convinced physicists light is a wave. The fringe-spacing formula is not in the IAL specification (the related grating equation \(n\lambda = d\sin\theta\) is: see Refraction, Diffraction & Polarisation), but it falls straight out of the path-difference rule.

Waves leaving the two slits at angle \(\theta\) have a path difference of \(a\sin\theta\). The \(n\)th bright fringe needs a path difference of \(n\lambda\):

\[ a\sin\theta = n\lambda \]

The screen is far away compared with the fringe positions, so \(\theta\) is small and \(\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta = x_n / D\), where \(x_n\) is the distance of the \(n\)th fringe from the centre:

\[ a\,\frac{x_n}{D} = n\lambda \quad\Rightarrow\quad x_n = \frac{n\lambda D}{a} \]

Neighbouring fringes (\(n\) and \(n+1\)) are separated by \(x = \lambda D / a\), so

\[ \boxed{\lambda = \frac{a x}{D}} \]

Example: \(a = 0.50\) mm, \(D = 2.0\) m and fringe spacing \(x = 2.6\) mm give \(\lambda = 6.5 \times 10^{-7}\) m, which is red light.

Stationary waves: how they form

Flick one end of a rope tied to a wall and the pulse comes back, reflected. Keep shaking steadily and you now have two waves on the rope at once: the one you are sending and its reflection, with the same frequency, speed and (nearly) the same amplitude, travelling in opposite directions. Their superposition is something new. The wave pattern no longer moves along the rope. Some points never move at all, while others in between swing up and down with a large amplitude. This is a stationary (or standing) wave.

Stationary waves, nodes and antinodes

  • Stationary wave: the wave formed when two progressive waves of the same frequency (and similar amplitude) travelling in opposite directions superpose. It stores energy rather than transferring it.
  • Node: a point on a stationary wave with zero amplitude (no displacement at any time).
  • Antinode: a point on a stationary wave with maximum amplitude.

Why there are nodes, and why they are λ/2 apart

At a node, the two travelling waves always arrive in antiphase, so they cancel at every instant. At an antinode they always arrive in phase and add to give twice the amplitude of either wave.

Move a distance \(\Delta x\) along. The wave travelling one way now has to travel \(\Delta x\) further and the other \(\Delta x\) less, so their phase difference changes by \(2 \times 2\pi\Delta x/\lambda\). To get from one antiphase point to the next, that change must be \(2\pi\), which happens when \(\Delta x = \lambda/2\). So adjacent nodes are \(\lambda/2\) apart, with an antinode halfway between, \(\lambda/4\) from each node.

You can make stationary waves with any kind of wave, as long as two waves of the same frequency travel through each other in opposite directions. The usual trick is reflection:

  • a string fixed at both ends and vibrated (a guitar, or the vibration generator in Core Practical 5);
  • microwaves from a transmitter reflected back by a metal plate, detected with a probe moved along the line between them;
  • sound from a loudspeaker reflected from a wall or from the closed end of a tube.

The maths of a stationary wave

Writing the two travelling waves as sine functions shows exactly why the pattern stands still.

A wave moving in the \(+x\) direction and an identical one moving in the \(-x\) direction (with \(k = 2\pi/\lambda\) and \(\omega = 2\pi f\)):

\[ y_1 = A\sin(kx - \omega t), \qquad y_2 = A\sin(kx + \omega t) \]

Superpose them and use the identity \(\sin P + \sin Q = 2\sin\frac{P+Q}{2}\cos\frac{P-Q}{2}\):

\[ y = y_1 + y_2 = 2A\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t) \]

The position and the time have separated. \(\cos\omega t\) makes every point oscillate at the same frequency, and \(2A\sin kx\) sets each point's amplitude. Nodes are where \(\sin kx = 0\):

\[ kx = n\pi \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{x = \frac{n\lambda}{2}} \]

\(\sin kx\) changes sign at each node, which is why neighbouring loops are in antiphase.

Graphs of stationary waves

A stationary wave is usually drawn as an envelope: the two extreme positions of the string, one solid and one dashed. If a question asks for the shape at particular instants, draw snapshots. Start the clock (\(t = 0\)) when the string is at its full displacement. A quarter of a period later (\(t = T/4\)) it is momentarily completely straight, with every point rushing through equilibrium at its maximum speed, and at \(t = T/2\) it is fully displaced the other way. The energy swaps between kinetic energy (string straight) and elastic potential energy (string at maximum displacement).

The phase pattern is completely different from a progressive wave. All the points between two neighbouring nodes reach their maximum displacement at the same moment, so they are in phase, even though their amplitudes are different. Points in adjacent loops are in antiphase: when one loop is up, the next is down. Look at the red and blue particles in the simulation.

Worked example

A microwave transmitter faces a flat metal plate. A detector moved along the line between them finds a series of maxima and minima. Moving the detector from one minimum to the sixth minimum after it covers a distance of 8.4 cm.
(a) Explain why the minima occur.
(b) Calculate the frequency of the microwaves.
(c) Explain why the signal at each minimum is small but not zero.

Show solution

(a) Microwaves reflect from the plate and travel back towards the transmitter. The incident and reflected waves have the same frequency and travel in opposite directions, so they superpose to form a stationary wave. The minima are the nodes, where the two waves are always in antiphase and cancel.

(b) The starting minimum and the sixth one after it are 6 node-to-node gaps apart, and each gap is \(\lambda/2\):

\[ 6 \times \frac{\lambda}{2} = 8.4\ \text{cm} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \lambda = 2.8\ \text{cm} = 0.028\ \text{m} \] \[ f = \frac{c}{\lambda} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^{8}}{0.028} = 1.1 \times 10^{10}\ \text{Hz}\ (2\ \text{s.f.}) \]

(c) The reflected wave has a smaller amplitude than the incident wave (some energy is absorbed and it spreads out), so the two cannot cancel completely at the nodes.

Stationary waves in pipes and wind instruments

Air in a tube can carry a stationary sound wave. The end conditions decide which wavelengths fit. A closed end is a displacement node (the air can't move into the wall) and an open end is close to a displacement antinode.

  • Open at both ends: antinodes at both ends, so \(L = n\lambda/2\) and \(f_n = nv/2L\): every harmonic, like a string.
  • Closed at one end: node at one end, antinode at the other, so \(L = \lambda/4, 3\lambda/4, \dots\) and \(f = nv/4L\) with \(n\) odd only. This is why a clarinet sounds so different from a flute.

The antinode actually sits slightly beyond an open end (an "end correction" of roughly 0.6 × the tube's radius), which is why resonance-tube experiments measure the difference between two resonance lengths.

Waves on a string: v = √(T/μ)

Pluck a guitar string and a wave travels along it, reflects off the ends and sets up a stationary wave. How fast does the wave travel? It depends only on two properties of the string.

Formula sheet

Speed of a transverse wave on a string

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} \]
\(v\): wave speed / m s⁻¹\(T\): tension / N\(\mu\): mass per unit length / kg m⁻¹
  • Tension provides the restoring force. A tighter string snaps back more quickly when it is displaced, so the disturbance is passed on faster: \(v\) goes up with \(T\).
  • Mass per unit length \(\mu\) is the string's inertia. A heavier string responds more sluggishly to the same force, so \(v\) goes down as \(\mu\) goes up. For a wire, \(\mu = m/l\) (mass ÷ length), which is also density × cross-sectional area.
  • The speed does not depend on the frequency or amplitude of the wave. Change \(f\) and the wavelength changes instead, so that \(v = f\lambda\) still holds.

A quick units check shows the equation is homogeneous: \(\sqrt{\text{N} / \text{kg m}^{-1}} = \sqrt{\text{kg m s}^{-2} \times \text{m kg}^{-1}} = \sqrt{\text{m}^2\,\text{s}^{-2}} = \text{m s}^{-1}\).

Harmonics: which stationary waves fit?

A string clamped at both ends must have a node at each end. So a stationary wave can only form if a whole number of loops (half-wavelengths) fits exactly into the length \(L\). Each of these patterns is a harmonic, and the string resonates only at their frequencies.

Both ends are nodes, and each loop is half a wavelength. With \(n\) loops on a string of length \(L\):

\[ L = n\,\frac{\lambda}{2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \lambda = \frac{2L}{n} \]

The wave speed on the string is fixed by \(T\) and \(\mu\), so use \(v = f\lambda\) to find the frequency:

\[ f = \frac{v}{\lambda} = \frac{n v}{2L} \]

Substitute \(v = \sqrt{T/\mu}\):

\[ \boxed{f_n = \frac{n}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}} \]

For the fundamental, \(n = 1\):

\[ f_1 = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} \]

So \(f_1 \propto 1/L\), \(f_1 \propto \sqrt{T}\) and \(f_1 \propto 1/\sqrt{\mu}\): exactly the three relationships Core Practical 5 tests. Short, tight, thin strings give high notes.

The \(\lambda\) in these equations is the wavelength of the wave on the string, not of the sound it makes in the air. The frequency is the same in both, but the sound travels at about 340 m s⁻¹, so its wavelength is different.

Worked example

A guitar string has a vibrating length of 0.648 m and a mass per unit length of 1.15 × 10⁻³ kg m⁻¹. It plays a fundamental frequency of 110 Hz.
(a) Calculate the speed of waves on the string.
(b) Calculate the tension in the string.
(c) The player presses the string against a fret so that the vibrating length becomes 0.432 m. Calculate the new fundamental frequency.
(d) The tension is then increased by 10%. By what factor does the frequency change?

Show solution

(a) For the fundamental, \(\lambda = 2L = 2 \times 0.648 = 1.296\ \text{m}\):

\[ v = f\lambda = 110 \times 1.296 = 142.6\ \text{m s}^{-1} \approx 143\ \text{m s}^{-1} \]

(b) Square \(v = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) and rearrange:

\[ T = \mu v^2 = 1.15 \times 10^{-3} \times 142.6^2 = 23.4\ \text{N}\ (3\ \text{s.f.}) \]

(c) \(T\) and \(\mu\) are unchanged, so \(v\) is unchanged and \(f_1 \propto 1/L\):

\[ f_1 = \frac{v}{2L} = \frac{142.6}{2 \times 0.432} = 165\ \text{Hz}\ (3\ \text{s.f.}) \]

(d) \(f \propto \sqrt{T}\), so the frequency changes by a factor of \(\sqrt{1.10} = 1.049\): it rises by about 4.9%.

Why is the speed √(T/μ)?

You only need to use this equation, but there is a neat argument using circular motion (which you'll meet properly in Unit 4) that shows where it comes from.

Ride along with a small pulse moving at speed \(v\). In your frame the pulse is still and the string streams through it at speed \(v\).

Look at a tiny piece of string of length \(\Delta l\) at the top of the pulse. It follows a circular arc of radius \(R\), so it is moving in a circle, and its mass is \(\mu\,\Delta l\).

The tension pulls along the string at each end. Each pull is tilted towards the centre of the circle by a small angle \(\theta\), where \(\Delta l = 2R\theta\). The net inward force is

\[ 2T\sin\theta \approx 2T\theta = \frac{T\,\Delta l}{R} \]

That net force provides the centripetal force \(mv^2/R\):

\[ \frac{T\,\Delta l}{R} = \frac{(\mu\,\Delta l)\,v^2}{R} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}} \]

\(\Delta l\) and \(R\) cancel, so every pulse travels at the same speed whatever its shape or size, as long as the displacement is small.

Core Practical 5: frequency of a vibrating string

Investigate the effects of length, tension and mass per unit length on the frequency of a vibrating string or wire

Apparatus: a string or wire fixed at one end to a vibration generator (driven by a signal generator), passing over a bridge and a pulley at the other end, with a mass hanger hanging from it to set the tension \(T = mg\). A second movable bridge can set the vibrating length \(L\).

  1. Set the length, tension and wire. Slowly increase the signal generator frequency from a low value until the string vibrates in one loop with the largest amplitude: this is the fundamental \(f_1\). Read \(f\) from the signal generator.
  2. Length: keep \(T\) and \(\mu\) fixed and repeat for at least 6 lengths, measured between the bridges (nodes) with a metre rule. Plot \(f_1\) against \(1/L\): a straight line through the origin with gradient \(= \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{T/\mu} = v/2\).
  3. Tension: keep \(L\) and \(\mu\) fixed and add masses. Plot \(f_1\) against \(\sqrt{T}\): gradient \(= 1/(2L\sqrt{\mu})\).
  4. Mass per unit length: keep \(L\) and \(T\) fixed and use different wires. Find each \(\mu\) by weighing a measured length of the same wire on a top-pan balance (\(\mu = m/l\)), or from its density and diameter measured with a micrometer. Plot \(f_1\) against \(1/\sqrt{\mu}\): gradient \(= \sqrt{T}/(2L)\).
  • Judging resonance is the main source of uncertainty, because the amplitude is large over a range of frequencies. Approach the resonance from below and from above, note both frequencies and average them. A strobe lamp makes it easier to see when the loops are largest. You can also find a higher harmonic and divide its frequency by \(n\), which can reduce the percentage uncertainty in \(f_1\).
  • The end at the vibration generator is not a perfect node, so measure \(L\) between the nodes you can see, not to the generator.
  • Use the mass of the hanger in \(T\), and a low-friction pulley so the tension really is \(mg\). The wire stretches slightly as the tension increases, which changes \(\mu\) a little.
  • Safety: wear eye protection in case the wire snaps, keep feet clear of the hanging masses (place a box of sand or a cushion underneath), and don't run the vibration generator above its rated current.
Worked example

In Core Practical 5 a student keeps the tension at 4.9 N and plots \(f_1\) against \(1/L\). The best-fit line passes through the origin with gradient 47.5 Hz m. Calculate the mass per unit length of the wire.

Show solution

Since \(f_1 = \dfrac{v}{2} \times \dfrac{1}{L}\), the gradient is \(v/2\):

\[ v = 2 \times 47.5 = 95.0\ \text{m s}^{-1} \]

Then from \(v = \sqrt{T/\mu}\):

\[ \mu = \frac{T}{v^2} = \frac{4.9}{95.0^2} = 5.4 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{kg m}^{-1}\ (2\ \text{s.f.}) \]

Quick checks

Two points on a progressive wave are 0.15 m apart along the direction of travel. The wavelength is 0.60 m. What is the phase difference between them?

The points are \(0.15/0.60 = 0.25\) of a wavelength apart, so \(\phi = 0.25 \times 2\pi = \pi/2\) rad. "0.25 rad" forgets to multiply the fraction of a cycle by \(2\pi\).

Which of these is needed for two sources to be coherent?

Coherence is about a steady phase relationship, which needs equal frequencies. The amplitudes can differ (you just won't get complete cancellation), and the phase difference can be any value as long as it doesn't change.

Two loudspeakers are connected so that they vibrate in antiphase. A microphone is placed at a point exactly the same distance from both. What does it detect?

With zero path difference the waves arrive with the same phase difference they left with: \(\pi\). Antiphase sources are still coherent, because the phase difference is constant.

Points X and Y lie between the same pair of adjacent nodes on a stationary wave. X is closer to the antinode. Which statement is correct?

Within one loop of a stationary wave every point moves up and down together (in phase), but the amplitude increases from zero at the nodes to a maximum at the antinode.

On a stationary wave of wavelength λ, what is the distance between a node and the nearest antinode?

Adjacent nodes are \(\lambda/2\) apart and the antinode lies halfway between them, so it is \(\lambda/4\) from each node.

The tension in a vibrating string is made four times bigger. The length and the string are unchanged. What happens to the fundamental frequency?

The wavelength of the fundamental is fixed at \(2L\), but \(v = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) doubles when \(T\) is multiplied by 4. Since \(f = v/\lambda\), the frequency doubles too.
