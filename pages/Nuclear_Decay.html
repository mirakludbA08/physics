Nuclear Decay

The nucleus holds enormous amounts of energy, and this page is about how it gets out. You will see why a nucleus weighs less than the parts it is made from, why both splitting heavy nuclei and joining light ones release energy, what alpha, beta and gamma radiation are, and why a lump of radioactive material decays in such a predictable way when each nucleus inside it behaves completely at random.

Mass deficit and binding energy

A nucleus is made of protons and neutrons (together called nucleons) held together by the strong nuclear force. Weigh a helium-4 nucleus and compare it with two protons plus two neutrons weighed separately, and something odd turns up: the nucleus is lighter than its parts. This missing mass is the mass deficit (also called the mass defect).

Where has it gone? When nucleons are pulled together by the strong force they lose potential energy, just as a ball loses potential energy when it falls, and that energy is given out. Einstein showed that energy and mass are equivalent, \(\Delta E = c^2\Delta m\), so a system that has given out energy has less mass. To pull the nucleus apart again you would have to put all of that energy back. That energy is the binding energy.

Mass deficit and binding energy

The mass deficit of a nucleus is the difference between the total mass of its separate protons and neutrons and the mass of the nucleus itself.

The binding energy of a nucleus is the energy needed to separate it completely into its individual protons and neutrons. It equals the energy released when the nucleus forms from them, and \(\text{binding energy} = c^2 \times \text{mass deficit}\).

Formula sheet

Mass–energy equivalence

\[ \Delta E = c^2\,\Delta m \]
\(\Delta E\): energy / J\(c\): speed of light, 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹\(\Delta m\): change in mass / kg

Because \(c^2 = 9.00 \times 10^{16}\ \text{m}^2\,\text{s}^{-2}\) is so huge, a tiny mass is worth a vast amount of energy. One gram of mass is equivalent to \(9 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}\), roughly the energy released by the atomic bomb dropped on Hiroshima.

Binding energy is not energy that the nucleus contains and can give out. It is the energy you would have to supply to break the nucleus up. A nucleus with a large binding energy has a large mass deficit: it is in a deep energy “hole”, which makes it more stable, not more energetic.

The atomic mass unit

Nuclear masses are tiny in kilograms, so we use the atomic mass unit, u, defined as one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom:

\[ 1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg} \]

A proton is 1.00728 u and a neutron 1.00867 u. To convert a mass in u to kg, multiply by \(1.66 \times 10^{-27}\). The energy equivalent of 1 u, using the data-sheet values, is \(c^2 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 1.49 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{J}\), which is about 930 MeV (1 MeV = \(1.60 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}\)).

For a binding-energy calculation: (1) add up the masses of the separate nucleons, (2) subtract the mass of the nucleus to get \(\Delta m\) in u, (3) convert \(\Delta m\) to kg, (4) use \(\Delta E = c^2\Delta m\) to get joules, (5) divide by \(1.60 \times 10^{-13}\) if you want MeV. Keep every decimal place in step 2: you are subtracting two nearly equal numbers, so rounding early wipes out the answer.

Worked example

A helium-4 nucleus has a mass of 4.00151 u. Calculate (a) its mass deficit in kg, (b) its binding energy in MeV, and (c) its binding energy per nucleon. (proton mass = 1.00728 u, neutron mass = 1.00867 u)

Show solution

Total mass of 2 protons and 2 neutrons:

\[ 2(1.00728) + 2(1.00867) = 4.03190\ \text{u} \]

(a) \(\Delta m = 4.03190 - 4.00151 = 0.03039\ \text{u} = 0.03039 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 5.04 \times 10^{-29}\ \text{kg}\)

(b) \(\Delta E = c^2\Delta m = (3.00 \times 10^{8})^2 \times 5.045 \times 10^{-29} = 4.54 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{J}\)

\[ \frac{4.54 \times 10^{-12}}{1.60 \times 10^{-13}} = 28.4\ \text{MeV} \]

(c) There are 4 nucleons, so the binding energy per nucleon is \(28.4 / 4 = 7.1\ \text{MeV}\).

Binding energy per nucleon: fission and fusion

Big nuclei have more nucleons, so they always have a bigger total binding energy. To compare how tightly different nuclei are held together, divide by the nucleon number. The binding energy per nucleon is the average energy needed to remove one nucleon. The higher it is, the more stable the nucleus.

Plot it against nucleon number and you get one of the most important graphs in physics. It rises steeply for light nuclei, because each extra nucleon has more neighbours for the strong force to bind it to. It peaks at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon around iron-56, the most stable region. Then it falls slowly for heavy nuclei: the strong force only reaches nearest neighbours, but every proton repels every other proton across the whole nucleus, and that repulsion grows as the nucleus gets bigger.

Up the curve means energy out

Any nuclear reaction that ends with the nucleons higher up the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve releases energy, because the products are more tightly bound and so have less mass.

  • Fusion: two light nuclei join to make a heavier one, moving up the steep left-hand side.
  • Fission: a heavy nucleus (such as uranium-235, after absorbing a neutron) splits into two medium-sized nuclei, moving up the gentle right-hand side.

Energy released = total binding energy after − total binding energy before = \(c^2 \times\) (mass lost). Neither process can release energy beyond iron: fusing iron or splitting it would both move down the curve.

Fusion releases more energy per nucleon, because the left-hand side of the curve is so steep. Fission releases more energy per reaction (about 200 MeV against 17.6 MeV for deuterium–tritium fusion), because so many nucleons each gain a little.

Worked example

In a nuclear reactor, uranium-235 absorbs a neutron and splits: \({}^{235}_{92}\text{U} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow {}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n}\). The binding energies per nucleon are U-235: 7.59 MeV, Ba-141: 8.33 MeV, Kr-92: 8.51 MeV. (a) Calculate the energy released in one fission. (b) Estimate the energy released if 1.0 kg of uranium-235 undergoes fission.

Show solution

(a) Free neutrons have no binding energy, so only the nuclei count.

\[ \text{before: } 235 \times 7.59 = 1784\ \text{MeV} \] \[ \text{after: } 141 \times 8.33 + 92 \times 8.51 = 1174.5 + 782.9 = 1957\ \text{MeV} \] \[ \text{energy released} = 1957 - 1784 = 174\ \text{MeV} = 174 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-13} = 2.78 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{J} \]

(More energy, bringing the total to about 200 MeV, is released later as the neutron-rich fission products decay.)

(b) Mass of one U-235 nucleus \(\approx 235 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 3.90 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{kg}\), so 1.0 kg contains

\[ N = \frac{1.0}{3.90 \times 10^{-25}} = 2.56 \times 10^{24}\ \text{nuclei} \] \[ E = 2.56 \times 10^{24} \times 2.78 \times 10^{-11} = 7.1 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J} \]

Burning 1 kg of coal releases about \(3 \times 10^{7}\ \text{J}\), so this is over two million times more.

Making fusion happen

Fusion sounds easy: push two light nuclei together. The problem is that both are positively charged, so they repel. The strong force that could bind them only works over about \(10^{-15}\ \text{m}\) (1 femtometre, fm), and at that range the electrostatic repulsion is enormous. Two protons 3 fm apart push on each other with a force of about 26 N, which is huge for something with a mass of \(10^{-27}\ \text{kg}\).

To get that close, the nuclei must collide with enough kinetic energy to climb this electrostatic “hill”, often called the Coulomb barrier. The potential energy at 3 fm is

\[ E_\text{p} = \frac{Q_1Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \frac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times (1.60 \times 10^{-19})^2}{3 \times 10^{-15}} = 7.7 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{J} \approx 0.5\ \text{MeV} \]

That is why fusion needs two extreme conditions:

  • A very high temperature (tens of millions of kelvin or more), so the nuclei move fast enough. The average kinetic energy of a particle is \(\tfrac{3}{2}kT\) (see the Thermodynamics page), so hotter really does mean faster. At these temperatures the electrons are stripped off and the matter is a plasma of bare nuclei and electrons.
  • A very high density, so collisions happen extremely often. Even when it is very hot, only a tiny fraction of collisions are energetic enough to fuse, so you need an enormous number of collisions every second to get a useful rate. The energy released then heats the surrounding nuclei, which keeps the temperature up so that fusion can continue.

In a star, gravity squeezes the core to huge densities and holds the hot plasma together. On Earth, no material container can touch plasma at \(10^{8}\ \text{K}\), so reactors such as tokamaks hold it with strong magnetic fields, and keeping it dense and hot for long enough is the main engineering challenge.

Worked example

Estimate the temperature needed for two deuterium nuclei to get within 3.0 fm of each other, assuming the energy available in a collision is about \(\tfrac{3}{2}kT\).

Show solution

The energy needed is the electrostatic potential energy at 3.0 fm (each deuterium nucleus has charge \(+e\)):

\[ E_\text{p} = \frac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times (1.60 \times 10^{-19})^2}{3.0 \times 10^{-15}} = 7.67 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{J} \]

Set \(\tfrac{3}{2}kT = E_\text{p}\):

\[ T = \frac{2E_\text{p}}{3k} = \frac{2 \times 7.67 \times 10^{-14}}{3 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23}} = 3.7 \times 10^{9}\ \text{K} \]

This is only an estimate, but it shows that temperatures of billions of kelvin are needed if every collision has just the average energy.

How the Sun manages at only 15 million K

The Sun's core is about \(1.5 \times 10^{7}\ \text{K}\), a hundred times cooler than the estimate above, and yet it fuses hydrogen. Two things help. First, the speeds in a hot gas are spread out (the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution), so a few nuclei have many times the average energy. Second, quantum mechanics lets a nucleus tunnel through the barrier even when it doesn't have enough energy to go over the top. The chance is tiny for each collision, but with around \(10^{57}\) protons in the Sun, it is enough. The simulation above ignores tunnelling, which is why it needs higher temperatures than real stars do.

Alpha, beta and gamma radiation

An unstable nucleus becomes more stable by emitting radiation. There are three main kinds, and the differences between them all follow from what each one is.

RadiationWhat it isChargeIonising abilityRange in airStopped by
alpha, αhelium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons, \({}^{4}_{2}\alpha\)+2every stronga few cma sheet of paper, or the outer layer of skin
beta-minus, β⁻fast electron emitted from the nucleus, \({}^{\;\,0}_{-1}\beta\)−emoderateup to about a metrea few mm of aluminium
gamma, γhigh-frequency electromagnetic radiation (photons)0weakvery long; intensity falls with distancenever completely; several cm of lead or metres of concrete reduce it a lot

Why are the strongest ionisers the least penetrating? Each time radiation knocks an electron off an atom it loses a little energy (about 30 eV in air). An alpha particle is heavy, slow and doubly charged, so it interacts with almost every atom it passes, making tens of thousands of ion pairs per centimetre. It spends its few MeV within a few centimetres. A beta particle has less charge and moves much faster, so it interacts less often and gets further. A gamma photon has no charge and only interacts occasionally, so it penetrates a long way but ionises weakly.

That is also why alpha sources are fairly safe outside the body (dead skin stops them) but very dangerous if breathed in or swallowed, where all that ionisation happens in living cells.

Writing nuclear equations

Each nucleus is written \({}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\): the nucleon number \(A\) on top and the proton number \(Z\) underneath. In every nuclear equation, the totals of \(A\) and of \(Z\) (charge) must be the same on both sides.

  • Alpha decay: the nucleus loses 2 protons and 2 neutrons, so \(A\) falls by 4 and \(Z\) by 2: \[ {}^{226}_{\ 88}\text{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{222}_{\ 86}\text{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha \]
  • Beta-minus decay: a neutron turns into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino (\(\text{udd} \rightarrow \text{uud}\) in quark terms). \(A\) stays the same and \(Z\) goes up by 1: \[ {}^{14}_{\ 6}\text{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{\ 7}\text{N} + {}^{\;\,0}_{-1}\beta + \bar{\nu}_e \]
  • Beta-plus decay: a proton turns into a neutron, a positron and a neutrino. \(Z\) goes down by 1: \[ {}^{22}_{11}\text{Na} \rightarrow {}^{22}_{10}\text{Ne} + {}^{\;\,0}_{+1}\beta + \nu_e \]
  • Gamma emission: a nucleus left in an excited state (shown with a *) after alpha or beta decay gets rid of its extra energy as a photon. \(A\) and \(Z\) don't change: \[ {}^{60}_{28}\text{Ni}^{*} \rightarrow {}^{60}_{28}\text{Ni} + \gamma \]

The same rules apply to fission and fusion. For example, deuterium–tritium fusion is \({}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{3}_{1}\text{H} \rightarrow {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n}\): the top numbers give 5 = 5 and the bottom ones 2 = 2.

Worked example

Uranium-238 (\(Z = 92\)) decays by emitting an alpha particle, and the product then emits two beta-minus particles. (a) Identify the final nucleus. (b) Uranium-238 eventually becomes stable lead-206 (\(Z = 82\)). How many alpha and beta-minus decays happen along the way?

Show solution

(a) Alpha: \(A = 238 - 4 = 234\), \(Z = 92 - 2 = 90\) (thorium-234). Each beta-minus adds 1 to \(Z\): \(90 \rightarrow 91 \rightarrow 92\). The final nucleus is \({}^{234}_{\ 92}\text{U}\), a different isotope of uranium.

(b) Only alpha decay changes \(A\), by 4 each time: \(238 - 206 = 32\), so there are \(32/4 = 8\) alpha decays. Those would reduce \(Z\) by 16, from 92 to 76. To end at 82, \(Z\) must rise by 6, so there are 6 beta-minus decays.

The beta energy puzzle and the neutrino

In alpha decay every alpha particle from a given isotope comes out with the same energy. In beta decay the electrons come out with a whole range of energies, from almost zero up to a maximum. Energy seemed to be going missing. In 1930 Wolfgang Pauli suggested that an unseen, uncharged, almost massless particle was carrying off the rest. This is the (anti)neutrino in the equations above. It interacts so weakly that it wasn't detected until 1956, beside a nuclear reactor producing trillions of them every second.

Which nuclei are unstable? The N–Z plot and decay chains

Plot the number of neutrons \(N\) against the number of protons \(Z\) for every stable nucleus and they lie in a narrow band. For light nuclei \(N \approx Z\), but heavier nuclei need extra neutrons, whose strong-force attraction helps hold back the growing proton repulsion. A nucleus above the band has too many neutrons and tends to decay by β⁻. One below the band has too many protons and tends to decay by β⁺. Very heavy nuclei usually emit alpha particles.

Often one decay isn't enough to reach the band, so a nucleus passes through a decay chain. Uranium-238 goes through 14 steps (the 8 alphas and 6 betas from the worked example) before ending as lead-206. One step is radon-222, a gas that seeps out of rocks and is a major part of our background radiation.

Background radiation

Switch on a Geiger–Müller (GM) tube with no source anywhere near it and it still clicks every second or two. That is background radiation, and it comes from:

  • radon gas seeping out of rocks and soil (in many places the largest part);
  • rocks, soil and building materials such as granite and concrete;
  • cosmic rays from space, and the particles they create in the atmosphere;
  • food and drink, which contain small amounts of radioactive potassium-40 and carbon-14, so your own body is slightly radioactive;
  • medical uses such as X-rays and scans, and a small amount from nuclear power and past weapons tests.

Background is random and changes from place to place, so you can't just guess it. Before an experiment, remove all sources and measure the background count over a long time (several minutes), then work out the background count rate. Subtract it from every reading to get the corrected count rate, the part that comes from your source.

\[ \text{corrected count rate} = \text{measured count rate} - \text{background count rate} \]

Subtract count rates, not raw counts taken over different times. Convert both to counts per second (or both to counts per minute) first. Background matters most when the source count rate is low, for example behind thick lead or after many half-lives.

Worked example

With no source present, a GM tube records 186 counts in 10 minutes. A source is then placed in front of it and the tube records 1240 counts in 2 minutes. Calculate the corrected count rate in counts per second.

Show solution

Background count rate \(= 186 / 600\ \text{s} = 0.31\ \text{s}^{-1}\).

Measured count rate \(= 1240 / 120\ \text{s} = 10.33\ \text{s}^{-1}\).

Corrected count rate \(= 10.33 - 0.31 = 10.0\ \text{s}^{-1}\).

Core Practical 15: absorption of gamma radiation

Investigate the absorption of gamma radiation by lead

First measure the background count rate: with the source locked away, count for about 10 minutes with the GM tube and counter. Then clamp a gamma source (such as cobalt-60) and the GM tube a fixed distance apart, so nothing moves during the experiment. Count for a fixed time (100 s or more) with no lead in between. Then put lead sheets of increasing thickness \(x\) between them, measuring each thickness with a micrometer at several points and averaging, and count again each time.

Subtract the background from every reading and plot corrected count rate against thickness of lead. The count rate falls quickly at first and then more and more slowly. It never reaches zero: lead reduces gamma radiation but never stops all of it.

  • Decay is random, so counts fluctuate. Count for long enough to get several hundred counts (the uncertainty in a count \(N\) is about \(\sqrt{N}\)), and repeat and average.
  • Keep the distance fixed: gamma intensity also falls with distance from the source.
  • Safety: handle the source with long tongs, never with your hands. Point it away from people, keep it at arm's length, keep the time it is out of its lead-lined box short, and put it straight back afterwards. Lead is toxic, so wear gloves or wash your hands after handling the sheets.

The exponential absorption law

The practical asks you to investigate how gamma radiation is absorbed, but the spec doesn't include an equation for it. Here is the idea anyway. Each millimetre of lead absorbs the same fraction of the photons that reach it (each photon has a fixed chance of being absorbed per mm, just like a nucleus has a fixed chance of decaying per second). That gives an exponential, like radioactive decay:

\[ C = C_0\,e^{-\mu x} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \ln C = \ln C_0 - \mu x \]

So a graph of \(\ln C\) against \(x\) is a straight line with gradient \(-\mu\), where \(\mu\) is the absorption coefficient. The thickness that halves the count rate is \(x_{1/2} = \ln 2 / \mu\), about 1 cm of lead for cobalt-60's gamma rays.

Random decay and half-life

Radioactive decay is spontaneous: nothing outside the nucleus triggers it, and it isn't affected by temperature, pressure or chemical reactions. It is also random: there is no way to predict which nucleus will decay next, or when. Every undecayed nucleus of a given isotope has the same chance of decaying in the next second, no matter how long it has already survived. A nucleus doesn't “get old”.

Think of each nucleus as a die, rolled once every second, that decays if it shows a six. You can't say when any one die will roll a six. But roll 6000 dice and you can be confident about 1000 sixes will come up. In the same way, if each nucleus has a probability \(\lambda\) of decaying per second, then out of \(N\) nuclei about \(\lambda N\) decay each second. With the huge numbers of nuclei in any real sample, this prediction is extremely accurate.

Activity, decay constant and half-life

The activity \(A\) of a sample is the number of nuclei that decay per unit time. Its unit is the becquerel: 1 Bq = 1 decay per second.

The decay constant \(\lambda\) is the probability that a nucleus decays per unit time. Its unit is s⁻¹.

The half-life \(t_{1/2}\) of an isotope is the time taken for half of the undecayed nuclei in a sample to decay (equivalently, for the activity to halve).

Formula sheet

Activity

\[ A = \lambda N \]
\(A\): activity / Bq\(\lambda\): decay constant / s⁻¹\(N\): number of undecayed nuclei
Formula sheet

Rate of decay

\[ \frac{dN}{dt} = -\lambda N \]
\(dN/dt\): rate of change of \(N\) / s⁻¹

\(\dfrac{dN}{dt}\) means the rate at which \(N\) changes. It is negative because \(N\) goes down, and it is proportional to \(N\): the more nuclei there are, the faster they disappear. As \(N\) falls, the rate of decay falls with it. The result is exponential decay: in every equal interval of time, the same fraction of the remaining nuclei decays. This is the same maths as a capacitor discharging through a resistor.

To find a half-life from an \(N\)–\(t\) or \(A\)–\(t\) graph, read the starting value, find where it has halved and read the time. Then check from there to a quarter, and from a quarter to an eighth: each should take the same time. Averaging several of these gives a more reliable value. If you are given count rates, make sure background has been subtracted first.

The exponential decay equations

The solution of \(dN/dt = -\lambda N\) is an exponential. Since \(A = \lambda N\), the activity decays in exactly the same way:

Formula sheet

Number of undecayed nuclei

\[ N = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t} \]
\(N_0\): number at \(t = 0\)
Formula sheet

Activity

\[ A = A_0\,e^{-\lambda t} \]
\(A_0\): activity at \(t = 0\) / Bq
Formula sheet

Decay constant and half-life

\[ \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}} \]
\(t_{1/2}\): half-life / s

After one half-life, half of the nuclei are left: \(N = \tfrac{1}{2}N_0\) when \(t = t_{1/2}\). Put this into \(N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}\):

\[ \tfrac{1}{2}N_0 = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \tfrac{1}{2} = e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}} \]

Take the natural log of both sides. \(\ln\tfrac{1}{2} = -\ln 2\), and \(\ln(e^{x}) = x\):

\[ -\ln 2 = -\lambda t_{1/2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}}} \]

So a large decay constant means a short half-life. \(\ln 2 \approx 0.693\).

An exponential curve is hard to read accurately, so we turn it into a straight line by taking logs. The spec says you must be able to derive these log equations.

Start from the decay equation:

\[ N = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t} \]

Take the natural log of both sides:

\[ \ln N = \ln\!\left(N_0\,e^{-\lambda t}\right) \]

The log of a product is the sum of the logs, \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\):

\[ \ln N = \ln N_0 + \ln\!\left(e^{-\lambda t}\right) \]

\(\ln\) undoes \(e\), so \(\ln(e^{-\lambda t}) = -\lambda t\):

\[ \boxed{\ln N = \ln N_0 - \lambda t} \]

The same steps applied to \(A = A_0e^{-\lambda t}\) give the log equation for activity (and for count rate, once background has been subtracted):

\[ \boxed{\ln A = \ln A_0 - \lambda t} \]

Compare with \(y = mx + c\): plotting \(\ln N\) (or \(\ln A\)) on the \(y\)-axis against \(t\) gives a straight line with gradient \(-\lambda\) and \(y\)-intercept \(\ln N_0\).

Solving dN/dt = −λN with calculus

You only need to use the solution, but if you know some integration, here is where the exponential comes from.

Separate the variables: everything with \(N\) on one side, everything with \(t\) on the other.

\[ \frac{dN}{N} = -\lambda\,dt \]

Integrate both sides. The integral of \(1/N\) is \(\ln N\):

\[ \ln N = -\lambda t + C \]

At \(t = 0\), \(N = N_0\), so \(C = \ln N_0\). Rearranging and taking the exponential of both sides:

\[ \ln N - \ln N_0 = -\lambda t \quad\Rightarrow\quad N = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t} \]
Worked example

A hospital has a sample of iodine-131 of mass 2.0 μg. Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8.0 days. (a) Show that the sample contains about \(9 \times 10^{15}\) nuclei. (b) Calculate its activity. (c) Calculate its activity 30 days later.

Show solution

(a) Mass of one nucleus \(\approx 131 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 2.17 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{kg}\), so

\[ N = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-9}}{2.17 \times 10^{-25}} = 9.2 \times 10^{15} \]

(b) The half-life must be in seconds for an activity in Bq: \(t_{1/2} = 8.0 \times 24 \times 3600 = 6.91 \times 10^{5}\ \text{s}\).

\[ \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{6.91 \times 10^{5}} = 1.00 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{s}^{-1}, \qquad A = \lambda N = 1.00 \times 10^{-6} \times 9.2 \times 10^{15} = 9.2 \times 10^{9}\ \text{Bq} \]

(c) In days, \(\lambda = \ln 2 / 8.0 = 0.0866\ \text{day}^{-1}\), so \(\lambda t = 0.0866 \times 30 = 2.60\):

\[ A = A_0e^{-\lambda t} = 9.2 \times 10^{9} \times e^{-2.60} = 6.8 \times 10^{8}\ \text{Bq} \]

Notice you can use any time unit for \(\lambda t\), as long as \(\lambda\) and \(t\) use the same one.

Worked example

Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5730 years. A piece of ancient wood has a corrected count rate that is 30% of the rate from the same mass of living wood. Estimate the age of the wood.

Show solution

\(\lambda = \ln 2 / 5730 = 1.21 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{year}^{-1}\). Using the log equation with \(A/A_0 = 0.30\):

\[ \ln A = \ln A_0 - \lambda t \quad\Rightarrow\quad t = \frac{\ln(A_0/A)}{\lambda} = \frac{\ln(1/0.30)}{1.21 \times 10^{-4}} = 9.95 \times 10^{3}\ \text{years} \]

Check: 30% is between \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) (one half-life) and \(\tfrac{1}{4}\) (two half-lives), and 9950 years (about 10 000 years) is between 5730 and 11 460 years.

Quick checks

A radioactive sample is heated from 20 °C to 200 °C. What happens to its half-life?

Decay is spontaneous: it isn't affected by temperature, pressure or chemistry. Ordinary heating changes the motion of whole atoms, which is nowhere near enough to affect what happens inside the nucleus.

What fraction of a radioactive isotope remains undecayed after three half-lives?

Each half-life halves what is left: \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times \tfrac{1}{2} \times \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{8}\). It never drops to zero after a whole number of half-lives.

Why do alpha particles have such a short range in air?

Alpha particles are heavy, slow and have charge +2e, so they ionise almost every atom they pass. Each ionisation takes some energy, so their few MeV are used up within a few centimetres. (Alpha particles are usually emitted with more energy than beta particles, not less.)

Which of these reactions releases energy?

Energy is released when the products have a higher binding energy per nucleon. Fusing hydrogen-2 (about 1.1 MeV per nucleon) into helium-4 (7.1 MeV per nucleon) moves steeply up the curve. The other three all move down the curve, so they would need energy to be supplied.

Why must the fuel in a fusion reactor be at a very high temperature?

Nuclei are positive and repel. They have to get within about \(10^{-15}\ \text{m}\) for the short-range strong force to act, which needs enormous kinetic energy, and so an enormous temperature. (High density is needed too, but for a different reason: to make collisions frequent enough.)

A graph of ln(corrected count rate) against time is a straight line with gradient −0.0231 s⁻¹. What is the half-life?

The gradient is \(-\lambda\), so \(\lambda = 0.0231\ \text{s}^{-1}\) and \(t_{1/2} = \ln 2/\lambda = 0.693/0.0231 = 30\ \text{s}\). (43 s is \(1/\lambda\), which is not the half-life.)

Polonium-210 (\(Z = 84\)) decays by alpha emission. What is the product?

Alpha emission removes \({}^{4}_{2}\alpha\): \(A = 210 - 4 = 206\) and \(Z = 84 - 2 = 82\), which is lead. Check: \(210 = 206 + 4\) and \(84 = 82 + 2\).
