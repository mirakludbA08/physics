Astrophysics & Cosmology

We will never visit another star, yet we know how hot stars are, how big and how far away they are, and how they live and die. All of it comes from the light that reaches us: a star's spectrum gives its temperature, its brightness and distance give its power and size, and the redshift of distant galaxies reveals an expanding universe with a finite age.

Black-body radiation

Everything emits electromagnetic radiation because of its temperature. You can't see your own glow because at 37 °C it is all infrared, but heat a poker and it glows dull red, then orange, then yellow-white. The hotter an object is, the more it radiates and the shorter the wavelengths it radiates most strongly.

Black body

A black body is an object that absorbs all the electromagnetic radiation that falls on it. It is also the best possible emitter, and the spectrum it emits depends only on its temperature.

A black body only looks black when it is cold. Stars are very close to black bodies: their hot, dense gas absorbs any radiation falling on it, and the light they give out depends almost entirely on their surface temperature. That is how we read a star's temperature from its light.

Reading a black-body radiation curve

A radiation curve plots the intensity emitted at each wavelength. Every black-body curve has the same shape:

  • It is a continuous spectrum: some radiation at every wavelength, with a single peak at \(\lambda_\text{max}\).
  • It rises steeply on the short-wavelength side and has a long tail on the long-wavelength side.
  • A hotter body has a curve that is higher at every wavelength, so the curves for different temperatures never cross.
  • A hotter body has its peak at a shorter wavelength (Wien's law), and a much bigger area under the curve, which represents the total power emitted per square metre (Stefan–Boltzmann law).

When you sketch black-body curves for two temperatures, make the hotter curve higher at every wavelength and put its peak further to the left (shorter wavelength). Students often draw the curves crossing, which loses the mark.

Planck's radiation law

The exact shape of the curve was explained by Max Planck in 1900. He had to assume that energy is emitted in packets \(E = hf\), which started quantum physics. The intensity at wavelength \(\lambda\) is proportional to

\[ \frac{1}{\lambda^5}\cdot\frac{1}{e^{hc/\lambda kT} - 1} \]

The simulation above draws exactly this. With calculus you can show that the peak of this curve obeys Wien's law and that the total area under it is proportional to \(T^4\), which is the Stefan–Boltzmann law.

Luminosity and temperature

Luminosity

The luminosity \(L\) of a star is the total power it emits as electromagnetic radiation, at all wavelengths and in all directions. It is measured in watts. The Sun's luminosity is \(3.83\times10^{26}\) W.

The Stefan–Boltzmann law

Formula sheet

Stefan–Boltzmann law

\[ L = \sigma A T^4 \]
\(L\): luminosity / W\(\sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\,\text{K}^{-4}\)\(A\): surface area / m² (\(4\pi r^2\) for a star)\(T\): surface temperature / K

\(\sigma T^4\) is the power radiated by each square metre of surface, and \(A\) tells you how many square metres there are. Because of the fourth power, temperature matters enormously: double the temperature and each square metre gives out \(2^4 = 16\) times as much power. Since \(A = 4\pi r^2\) for a sphere, \(L \propto r^2T^4\): a star can be very luminous by being hot, by being huge, or both.

Temperatures must be in kelvin, \(A\) is the surface area \(4\pi r^2\) (not \(\pi r^2\), and \(r\) is the radius, not the diameter), and the fourth power applies to \(T\) only. On a calculator, type \(T\) then the power key, then 4.

Wien's law

Formula sheet

Wien's law

\[ \lambda_\text{max}\,T = 2.898\times10^{-3}\ \text{m K} \]
\(\lambda_\text{max}\): wavelength at the peak of the curve / m\(T\): surface temperature / K

The peak wavelength is inversely proportional to the temperature: double \(T\) and \(\lambda_\text{max}\) halves. Astronomers measure the spectrum of a star, find where the peak is, and get the surface temperature without going anywhere near it. This is why colour tells you temperature: red stars are cool (about 3000 K), yellow-white stars like the Sun are about 6000 K, and blue-white stars are 10 000 K or more.

Worked example

The Sun has luminosity \(3.83\times10^{26}\) W and radius \(6.96\times10^8\) m. Calculate its surface temperature and the wavelength at which its spectrum peaks.

Show solution

Rearrange \(L = \sigma 4\pi r^2 T^4\):

\[ T^4 = \frac{L}{4\pi r^2\sigma} = \frac{3.83\times10^{26}}{4\pi\times(6.96\times10^8)^2\times5.67\times10^{-8}} = 1.11\times10^{15}\ \text{K}^4 \] \[ T = (1.11\times10^{15})^{1/4} = 5770\ \text{K} \]

Then Wien's law:

\[ \lambda_\text{max} = \frac{2.898\times10^{-3}}{5770} = 5.02\times10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 502\ \text{nm} \]

That is green light, in the middle of the visible spectrum. The Sun gives out plenty of every visible colour, which is why sunlight looks white rather than green.

Worked example

A red supergiant has a luminosity of \(4.5\times10^{31}\) W and its spectrum peaks at \(8.5\times10^{-7}\) m. Calculate its radius and compare it with the Sun's (\(6.96\times10^8\) m).

Show solution

First the temperature, from Wien's law:

\[ T = \frac{2.898\times10^{-3}}{8.5\times10^{-7}} = 3410\ \text{K} \]

Then rearrange \(L = 4\pi r^2\sigma T^4\) for \(r\):

\[ r = \sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi\sigma T^4}} = \sqrt{\frac{4.5\times10^{31}}{4\pi\times5.67\times10^{-8}\times3410^4}} = 6.8\times10^{11}\ \text{m} \]

That is about 980 times the Sun's radius. Placed where the Sun is, it would reach most of the way to Jupiter's orbit. It is cooler than the Sun but 120 000 times more luminous because its surface area is a million times bigger.

Intensity and the inverse-square law

How bright a star looks depends on how much power it emits and how far away it is. As the light travels out, the same power is spread over a bigger and bigger sphere.

A star of luminosity \(L\) radiates equally in all directions. At a distance \(d\), all of that power passes through the surface of a sphere of radius \(d\):

\[ A = 4\pi d^2 \]

Intensity is power per unit area, measured in W m⁻²:

\[ \boxed{I = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2}} \]

Double the distance and the area is four times bigger, so the intensity is a quarter: the same inverse-square law as gravity.

Formula sheet

Intensity of radiation

\[ I = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2} \]
\(I\): intensity received / W m⁻²\(L\): luminosity of the source / W\(d\): distance from the source / m

For example, the Earth is \(1.50\times10^{11}\) m from the Sun, so sunlight arriving at the top of the atmosphere has intensity \(I = 3.83\times10^{26}/(4\pi\times(1.50\times10^{11})^2) = 1.35\times10^3\ \text{W m}^{-2}\). Measuring \(I\) is easy with a telescope and a detector. So if you know any two of \(I\), \(L\) and \(d\), you can find the third.

Measuring distances to stars and galaxies

Trigonometric parallax

Hold a finger up at arm's length and look at it with one eye, then the other. It jumps sideways against the background. A nearby star does the same as the Earth moves round the Sun: observed six months apart, from opposite sides of the Earth's orbit, it shifts slightly against the much more distant stars. The closer the star, the bigger the shift.

Parallax angle and the parsec

The parallax angle \(p\) is the angle subtended at the star by the radius of the Earth's orbit (1 AU). It is half the total angular shift of the star seen six months apart.

One parsec (pc) is the distance at which 1 AU subtends an angle of 1 arcsecond (1/3600 of a degree). 1 pc = \(3.09\times10^{16}\) m = 3.26 light-years.

The Sun, the Earth and the star make a right-angled triangle. The side opposite \(p\) is 1 AU, and the long side is the distance \(d\):

\[ \tan p = \frac{1\ \text{AU}}{d} \]

The angle is tiny, so \(\tan p \approx p\) with \(p\) in radians:

\[ d = \frac{1\ \text{AU}}{p} \]

1 arcsecond is \(\tfrac{1}{3600}\times\tfrac{\pi}{180} = 4.85\times10^{-6}\) rad, so a star with \(p\) = 1 arcsecond is at \(1.50\times10^{11}/4.85\times10^{-6} = 3.09\times10^{16}\) m, which is 1 pc. In these units:

\[ \boxed{d\ (\text{pc}) = \frac{1}{p\ (\text{arcseconds})}} \]
Worked example

Proxima Centauri, the nearest star to the Sun, has a parallax angle of 0.768 arcseconds. Calculate its distance in parsecs, metres and light-years. (1 light-year = \(9.46\times10^{15}\) m)

Show solution \[ d = \frac{1}{0.768} = 1.30\ \text{pc} \] \[ d = 1.30\times3.09\times10^{16} = 4.02\times10^{16}\ \text{m} = \frac{4.02\times10^{16}}{9.46\times10^{15}} = 4.25\ \text{light-years} \]

Parallax angles are tiny and the atmosphere blurs star images, so from the ground the method only works out to about 100 pc. Space telescopes such as Gaia reach thousands of parsecs, still only part of our own galaxy (about 30 000 pc across). Further away we need another method.

Standard candles

Standard candle

A standard candle is an astronomical object of known luminosity.

If you know how much power an object emits, measure the intensity \(I\) that arrives at Earth and rearrange \(I = L/4\pi d^2\):

\[ d = \sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi I}} \]

It is the same idea as judging how far away a car is at night from how bright its headlights look, because you know roughly how bright headlights are. Two important standard candles are Cepheid variable stars, which pulse in brightness, and type Ia supernovae, exploding white dwarfs so bright that they can be seen in galaxies billions of light-years away.

How do we know their luminosity? The distance ladder

In 1912 Henrietta Leavitt found that the longer a Cepheid takes to pulse, the more luminous it is, so its period gives its luminosity. The relationship is calibrated with nearby Cepheids whose distances are known from parallax, and Cepheids in turn calibrate type Ia supernovae. Each rung of this "distance ladder" depends on the one below, so errors build up, which matters for the Hubble constant.

Worked example

The star Sirius A has a parallax angle of 0.379 arcseconds. The intensity of its radiation at the Earth is \(1.17\times10^{-7}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\) and its spectrum peaks at 292 nm. Calculate (a) its distance, (b) its luminosity, (c) its surface temperature and (d) its radius.

Show solution

(a) \(d = 1/0.379 = 2.64\ \text{pc} = 2.64\times3.09\times10^{16} = 8.15\times10^{16}\ \text{m}\)

(b) Rearrange \(I = L/4\pi d^2\):

\[ L = 4\pi d^2 I = 4\pi\times(8.15\times10^{16})^2\times1.17\times10^{-7} = 9.77\times10^{27}\ \text{W} \]

(c) Wien's law:

\[ T = \frac{2.898\times10^{-3}}{292\times10^{-9}} = 9920\ \text{K} \]

(d) Stefan–Boltzmann law:

\[ r = \sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi\sigma T^4}} = \sqrt{\frac{9.77\times10^{27}}{4\pi\times5.67\times10^{-8}\times9920^4}} = 1.19\times10^9\ \text{m} \]

Sirius A is about 1.7 times the radius of the Sun and 25 times as luminous. Notice the chain: parallax gives \(d\), \(d\) and \(I\) give \(L\), the spectrum gives \(T\), and \(L\) and \(T\) give \(r\).

In these multi-step questions, convert parsecs or light-years to metres before using \(I = L/4\pi d^2\), and nanometres to metres before using Wien's law.

The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram

Around 1910 Ejnar Hertzsprung and Henry Russell plotted the luminosity of stars against their surface temperature. Instead of a random scatter, the stars fell into a few distinct groups.

  • Luminosity goes up the vertical axis, usually in multiples of the Sun's luminosity, on a logarithmic scale (each step is ten times the last).
  • Surface temperature goes along the horizontal axis, also on a log scale, and it decreases from left to right: hot blue stars on the left, cool red stars on the right.
RegionWhere on the diagramWhat the stars are like
Main sequencediagonal band from top left to bottom rightabout 90% of stars, including the Sun; fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores. Hotter ones are more massive and more luminous.
Red giantsabove the main sequence, on the rightcool (3000–5000 K) but 10 to 1000 times more luminous than the Sun, so they must be large
Supergiantsacross the very topthe most luminous stars, up to a million times the Sun; enormous
White dwarfsbottom lefthot but very dim, so they must be tiny: about the size of the Earth

Position on the diagram tells you the size

From \(L = 4\pi r^2\sigma T^4\), two stars with the same temperature but different luminosities must have different radii: \(r \propto \sqrt{L}\) at fixed \(T\). So stars at the top right are cool and very luminous, which means huge; stars at the bottom left are hot and dim, which means tiny. Lines of constant radius run diagonally across the diagram.

To sketch an HR diagram: label the axes "luminosity / \(L_\odot\)" (or in W) and "surface temperature / K", put the temperature scale the right way round (hot on the left, for example 40 000 K down to 3000 K), mark the main sequence as a band from top left to bottom right, add the red giants, supergiants and white dwarfs, and mark the Sun at about 6000 K and 1 \(L_\odot\).

Worked example

Sirius B is a white dwarf with surface temperature 25 000 K and luminosity \(2.1\times10^{25}\) W. Show that it is only a little larger than the Earth (radius \(6.37\times10^6\) m).

Show solution \[ r = \sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi\sigma T^4}} = \sqrt{\frac{2.1\times10^{25}}{4\pi\times5.67\times10^{-8}\times(25\,000)^4}} = 8.7\times10^6\ \text{m} \]

That is about 1.4 Earth radii, yet Sirius B has about the same mass as the Sun. It is hotter than the Sun but 18 times less luminous, because its surface area is so small.

The life cycle of stars

A star's whole life is a balance between gravity, pulling everything inwards, and pressure from the hot gas and radiation, pushing outwards. Which stages it goes through depends mainly on its mass.

  1. Nebula to protostar. A cloud of gas and dust, mostly hydrogen, collapses under its own gravity. Gravitational potential energy is converted into thermal energy and the core heats up.
  2. Main sequence. When the core reaches about \(10^7\) K, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium. The energy released keeps the core hot and the outward pressure balances gravity, so the star is stable for most of its life. More massive stars are hotter and more luminous and use up their hydrogen much faster: about 10 billion years for the Sun, only about 10 million years for a star of 15 solar masses.
  3. Giant stage. When the hydrogen in the core runs out, the core contracts and heats up, hydrogen fuses in a shell around it, and the outer layers swell up and cool. The star becomes a red giant (or, if it is massive, a red supergiant): it moves up and to the right on the HR diagram. The core gets hot enough to fuse helium into carbon and oxygen.
  4. Low-mass stars, like the Sun: the outer layers drift away as a glowing planetary nebula, leaving the hot core as a white dwarf. No fusion happens in it, so it slowly cools and fades, moving down and to the right on the HR diagram.
  5. High-mass stars: fusion continues, making heavier and heavier elements up to iron. Fusing iron releases no energy, so when the core is iron it suddenly collapses, and the outer layers are blasted away in a supernova. The core that remains becomes a neutron star, about 20 km across and as dense as an atomic nucleus, or, for the most massive stars, a black hole, whose gravity is so strong that not even light can escape.

Where are the dividing lines?

Stars that start with more than about 8 solar masses end in supernovae. A white dwarf is held up by its electrons resisting being squeezed closer, which only works up to about 1.4 solar masses (the Chandrasekhar limit). A heavier collapsing core is crushed into neutrons, and above roughly 2 to 3 solar masses even neutrons give way and a black hole forms. Every atom of carbon, oxygen and iron in your body was made inside a star.

The Doppler effect and redshift

An ambulance siren sounds higher as it comes towards you and lower as it moves away. The waves themselves travel at the same speed, but the source moves between emitting one crest and the next. Ahead of the source the crests bunch up (shorter wavelength, higher frequency); behind it they are stretched out (longer wavelength, lower frequency). This is the Doppler effect, and it happens for any wave, including light.

Redshift and blueshift

Light from a source moving away from us has its wavelengths stretched towards the red end of the spectrum: a redshift. A source moving towards us gives a blueshift. We can measure the shift very precisely because star and galaxy spectra contain absorption lines at wavelengths we know from the laboratory, like the hydrogen line at 656.3 nm. Compare where the lines are with where they should be and you get the speed.

A source moving away at speed \(v\) emits a crest, then the next crest one period \(T = 1/f\) later. In that time the first crest has travelled \(cT\) and the source has moved \(vT\) further away:

\[ \lambda_\text{observed} = cT + vT = \lambda + vT \]

So the change in wavelength is \(\Delta\lambda = vT\). Divide by \(\lambda = cT\):

\[ \frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} = \frac{vT}{cT} = \frac{v}{c} \]

This fractional change is called the redshift, \(z\). For speeds much less than \(c\) the fractional change in frequency is the same size, so

\[ \boxed{z = \frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} \approx \frac{\Delta f}{f} \approx \frac{v}{c}} \]

For a redshift the frequency goes down, so \(\Delta f\) is negative; the equation uses its size. The approximation only works when \(v \ll c\).

Formula sheet

Redshift of electromagnetic radiation

\[ z = \frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} \approx \frac{\Delta f}{f} \approx \frac{v}{c} \]
\(z\): redshift (no unit)\(\Delta\lambda\): observed minus emitted wavelength / m\(\lambda\): emitted (laboratory) wavelength / m\(v\): speed of the source along the line of sight / m s⁻¹
Worked example

The hydrogen line that is at 656.3 nm in the laboratory is observed at 671.9 nm in the spectrum of a distant galaxy. Calculate the redshift and the galaxy's speed. Is it moving towards us or away from us?

Show solution \[ z = \frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} = \frac{671.9 - 656.3}{656.3} = \frac{15.6}{656.3} = 0.0238 \] \[ v = zc = 0.0238\times3.00\times10^8 = 7.13\times10^6\ \text{m s}^{-1} \]

The wavelength has increased (redshift), so the galaxy is moving away from us, at about 2.4% of the speed of light.

When \(z\) gets big

\(z \approx v/c\) breaks down as \(v\) approaches \(c\). The most distant galaxies and quasars have \(z\) greater than 1, which does not mean they move faster than light. Special relativity gives \(1 + z = \sqrt{(1+v/c)/(1-v/c)}\) for a moving source, and for distant galaxies cosmologists think of the redshift as the light's wavelength being stretched by the expansion of space itself while the light travels to us.

Hubble's law and the age of the universe

In 1929 Edwin Hubble combined distances (from Cepheids) with redshifts for nearby galaxies. Almost every galaxy is moving away from us, and the further away it is, the faster it recedes. The recession velocity is proportional to distance:

Formula sheet

Hubble's law (cosmological expansion)

\[ v = H_0 d \]
\(v\): recession velocity / m s⁻¹ or km s⁻¹\(d\): distance / m or Mpc\(H_0\): the Hubble constant, about 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ \(= 2.3\times10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}\)

Why \(v \propto d\) means the universe is expanding

Picture a loaf of raisin bread rising in the oven. Every raisin moves away from every other one, and a raisin twice as far from you moves away twice as fast, because there is twice as much dough between you to stretch. Any raisin would see exactly the same thing, so we are not at the centre; there is no centre. Run the film backwards and everything was once much closer together: the evidence for the universe beginning in a hot, dense Big Bang.

Astronomers usually quote \(H_0\) in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ (1 Mpc = \(10^6\) pc = \(3.09\times10^{22}\) m). To convert to SI, write both parts in metres and seconds: \(70\ \text{km s}^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}^{-1} = 7.0\times10^4\ \text{m s}^{-1} \div 3.09\times10^{22}\ \text{m} = 2.27\times10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}\).

Suppose a galaxy now at distance \(d\) has been moving away from us at a constant speed \(v\) since the Big Bang. The time it has taken is

\[ t = \frac{d}{v} \]

Hubble's law says \(v = H_0 d\), so \(d\) cancels and the answer is the same for every galaxy:

\[ \boxed{t \approx \frac{1}{H_0}} \]

With \(H_0 = 2.27\times10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}\) and 1 year = \(3.15\times10^7\) s:

\[ t = \frac{1}{2.27\times10^{-18}} = 4.4\times10^{17}\ \text{s} = 1.4\times10^{10}\ \text{years} \]

The estimate assumes the expansion rate has always been the same. If gravity has slowed the expansion, galaxies used to move faster and the true age is less than \(1/H_0\).

Worked example

A type Ia supernova in the galaxy from the redshift example has a peak luminosity of \(2.0\times10^{36}\) W, and its peak intensity at the Earth is \(1.6\times10^{-14}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\). Use this and the galaxy's speed, \(7.13\times10^6\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), to find the Hubble constant and estimate the age of the universe.

Show solution

The supernova is a standard candle, so rearrange \(I = L/4\pi d^2\):

\[ d = \sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi I}} = \sqrt{\frac{2.0\times10^{36}}{4\pi\times1.6\times10^{-14}}} = 3.15\times10^{24}\ \text{m} \quad(\approx 100\ \text{Mpc}) \] \[ H_0 = \frac{v}{d} = \frac{7.13\times10^6}{3.15\times10^{24}} = 2.26\times10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1} \] \[ t \approx \frac{1}{H_0} = 4.42\times10^{17}\ \text{s} = \frac{4.42\times10^{17}}{3.15\times10^7} = 1.4\times10^{10}\ \text{years} \]

Why the value of \(H_0\) is controversial

Redshifts are easy to measure accurately; distances are not. For most of the 20th century different teams found values of \(H_0\) between about 50 and 100 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹, so the age \(1/H_0\) could be anywhere from about 10 to 20 billion years. At the high end the universe would be younger than the oldest stars in it, which is impossible. Today's measurements are much better, but small disagreements remain.

The "Hubble tension"

Measurements using Cepheids and supernovae give \(H_0 \approx 73\) km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹. Measurements based on the cosmic microwave background, the faint radiation left over from the early universe, give about 67 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹. The error bars no longer overlap, and nobody yet knows whether one method has a hidden error or the disagreement points to new physics.

Dark matter and the fate of the universe

Gravity pulls every galaxy towards every other one, so it slows the expansion down. What happens in the end depends on how much mass there is, or more precisely the average density of the universe:

  • Low density (open universe): gravity is too weak to stop the expansion, which continues for ever.
  • Critical density (flat universe): the expansion slows down, getting closer and closer to stopping but never quite reversing.
  • High density (closed universe): the expansion stops and reverses, and everything collapses back together in a "Big Crunch".

The missing mass: dark matter

To predict the fate we need the density, but most of the mass in the universe seems to be invisible. One strong piece of evidence comes from how galaxies rotate. Stars orbit the centre of a galaxy just as planets orbit the Sun, so their speed should obey \(v = \sqrt{GM/r}\), where \(M\) is the mass inside their orbit (see Gravitational Fields). Most of the visible stars are concentrated near the centre, so stars far out should move more slowly, like the outer planets. They don't: the orbital speed stays roughly constant far beyond the visible edge of the galaxy. There must be extra, unseen mass spread through a huge halo around it.

This dark matter does not emit or absorb light; we only detect it through its gravity. Measurements suggest there is roughly five times as much of it as ordinary matter, but nobody yet knows what it is.

The controversy in a nutshell

  • Age: \(t \approx 1/H_0\), so the age is only as good as our value of \(H_0\), and that depends on difficult distance measurements. If the expansion has slowed, the true age is less than \(1/H_0\).
  • Fate: it depends on the density of the universe compared with a critical density, and the critical density itself depends on \(H_0\).
  • Dark matter: most of the mass is invisible and of unknown nature, so the density, and therefore the fate, is hard to pin down.

Critical density and dark energy

The critical density is \(\rho_c = 3H_0^2/8\pi G\), about \(9\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg m}^{-3}\) (roughly five hydrogen atoms per cubic metre). In 1998, studies of distant type Ia supernovae found that the expansion is speeding up, driven by something named dark energy. In today's best model the universe is about 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter and 68% dark energy, is very close to flat, will expand for ever, and is 13.8 billion years old.

Quick checks

Star X has the same radius as star Y but twice the surface temperature. What is the luminosity of X divided by the luminosity of Y?

\(L = \sigma 4\pi r^2T^4\) with the same \(r\), so \(L \propto T^4 = 2^4 = 16\).

The spectrum of star P peaks at 300 nm and that of star Q peaks at 600 nm. Which statement is correct?

Wien's law: \(\lambda_\text{max} \propto 1/T\), so half the peak wavelength means twice the temperature. You can't say which is more luminous without knowing their radii.

A star appears in the top right-hand corner of a Hertzsprung–Russell diagram. What can you say about it?

The temperature axis runs from hot on the left to cool on the right, so the top right means low \(T\) but high \(L\). From \(L = 4\pi r^2\sigma T^4\), that needs a huge radius: a red giant or supergiant.

Star A has a parallax angle of 0.05 arcseconds and star B has a parallax angle of 0.10 arcseconds. Which statement is correct?

\(d = 1/p\): A is at 20 pc and B at 10 pc. A smaller parallax angle means a more distant star.

A spectral line at 486 nm in the laboratory is observed at 491 nm in light from a galaxy. What is the galaxy's velocity?

\(z = 5/486 = 0.0103\), so \(v = zc = 3.1\times10^6\ \text{m s}^{-1}\). The wavelength got longer (redshift), so the galaxy is receding.

A new measurement gives a larger value of the Hubble constant than before. What does this suggest about the universe?

The age is about \(1/H_0\), so a bigger \(H_0\) means a younger universe: galaxies are moving apart faster, so it took less time to reach their present separations.
